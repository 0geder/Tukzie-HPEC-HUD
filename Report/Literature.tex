\section{Scope and evaluation framework}
\label{sec:lit-scope}

The system combines four fields that are usually studied apart: ride and road sensing, embedded signal processing, vehicular telemetry, and driver-display design. The literature is judged here by what the TUKZIE Rev 0 needs, not only by reported accuracy: computational cost, sampling requirements, sensitivity to speed and mounting, real-time latency, communication loss and driver workload. A technique that works on a passenger car, a smartphone or a simulator may not transfer to a light three-wheeled cargo vehicle.

Sources were found by an automated search of OpenAlex, arXiv and Crossref on a fixed query set (ride characterisation, three-wheeler dynamics, cellular vehicle telemetry, MQTT, IMU calibration and fusion, camera hazard detection on edge hardware, driver displays and BMS protocols), run three times on 19 September 2026 and holding 733 records (\texttt{Research/research.db}), and by a targeted search of the three-wheeler market and open-source projects. The gap claim of Section~\ref{sec:lit-synthesis} is bounded by these searches.

Section~\ref{sec:lit-trikes} sets out why an electric cargo trike needs on-vehicle measurement. Sections~\ref{sec:lit-roughness} to~\ref{sec:lit-proximity} review ride response, inertial signal processing, edge architecture, driver displays, cellular synchronisation, camera detection and proximity sensing, and Section~\ref{sec:lit-platforms} compares hardware platforms. Section~\ref{sec:lit-synthesis} states the gap and the methods carried forward. Figure~\ref{fig:lit-map} shows which research question each theme informs.

\section{Electric cargo trikes and the case for telemetry}
\label{sec:lit-trikes}

Light electric freight vehicles can reduce the cost and emissions of urban delivery, by amounts that depend on route, payload, prices and the comparison vehicle. In Dar es Salaam an electric cargo tricycle had operating costs 45.5\% below a motorcycle and 86\% below a light car \cite{sebwa2025}; in Portland, Saenz et al.\ found greenhouse-gas reductions of 51 to 72\% for an electric-tricycle logistics service \cite{saenz2016}. Both figures came from measuring energy, distance and operations on vehicles in service, and an operator can confirm them for its own fleet only by measuring the same quantities, for which on-vehicle telemetry is the instrument. A three-wheeled chassis also makes vehicle response an engineering concern: payload position, tyre pressure, speed and surface affect both stability and measured vibration, so operating state should be recorded with the inertial data, and passenger-car roughness thresholds should not be transferred directly.

\section{Road roughness and ride response}
\label{sec:lit-roughness}

The International Roughness Index (IRI) is computed from a measured road profile through a standard quarter-car model \cite{fhwa2016}. An accelerometer on a vehicle measures something else: the combined response of road, tyres, suspension, chassis, payload, speed and mounting. Gonz\'alez et al.\ classified road profiles from simulated vehicle accelerations \cite{gonzalez2008}, but the inference depends on the assumed vehicle model, which cannot be carried over to a trike. Smartphone and low-cost studies extend the response-based approach, and identify placement, orientation, speed, sampling rate and inconsistent ground truth as persistent sources of variation \cite{sattar2018}; Alatoom and Smadi's microcontroller and single-board-computer framework is closer to this project because it allows controlled mounting and deterministic sampling \cite{alatoom2025}. None of these establishes a calibrated ride measure for a three-wheeled cargo vehicle. The defensible first claim for this project is therefore a repeatable, vehicle-specific response index and event log, validated by repeatability over traversals and sensitivity to speed and payload, not an IRI.

\section{Inertial signal processing and vibration separation}
\label{sec:lit-signal-processing}

An inertial signal contains gravity, orientation changes, road excitation, body motion, drivetrain vibration, noise and transients. Features from a nominally vertical axis are comparable only after orientation compensation, and become unreliable when samples are irregular or missing. Time-domain features (root-mean-square, standard deviation, peak-to-peak, crest factor, jerk, threshold counts) are cheap and interpretable; spectral energy and power spectral density separate sustained narrow-band vibration from broadband impacts. Bui et al.\ show rough-road detection running on an edge microcontroller \cite{bui2026}. Yiliguoqi et al.\ classified roughness with an SVM on vertical acceleration and roll features at only 10\,Hz, correctly in 80 to 100\% of trials across four vehicles \cite{yiliguoqi2026}; that range is tied to their phones, routes, labels and thresholds and cannot be expected on the TUKZIE without retraining. Learned classifiers should therefore follow a transparent statistical baseline, not replace calibration.

Separating road excitation from powertrain vibration needs an empirical baseline: stationary runs at several motor speeds identify repeatable powertrain components, and coast-down and repeated route runs show which components remain without traction and which change with the surface. Windowed statistics should be normalised or stratified by speed, because the same road feature excites the vehicle at a different frequency at a different speed \cite{li2018}. ISO~2631-1 defines the evaluation of whole-body vibration up to 80\,Hz \cite{iso2631}; its weighted measures describe comfort, which should be reported separately from vehicle response and road geometry.

\section{Edge computing and real-time telemetry architecture}
\label{sec:lit-edge}

Edge computing moves processing close to the sensors to reduce response time, bandwidth and dependence on a remote service \cite{shi2016}, which suits a vehicle whose alerts must work without coverage and whose raw 200\,Hz data would cost more to transmit than compact features; embedded HPEC also lets several streams be timestamped and fused on the vehicle \cite{barnell2018}. ``High performance'' here means predictable completion of time-critical work within a small power budget, not throughput. Threads alone do not give real-time behaviour: shared locks, blocking input and output, allocation and unbounded queues still cause missed samples or stale displays. The appropriate pattern is a producer and consumer design with timestamped samples in bounded queues, explicit overload behaviour (an old display frame may be dropped, an inertial sample must not be lost silently), and jitter, queue occupancy, display age and latency reported as distributions.

The closest comparison is an electric-racing telemetry system with ESP32 acquisition, Raspberry Pi~4B analytics and LoRaWAN, reporting 99\% packet delivery and sub-second latency on its track \cite{perezgonzalez2025}. Its division of acquisition from processing supports the architecture used here; its radio result is specific to its gateway and route. LTE gives wider reach at the cost of subscription, coverage dependence and less predictable delay, which argues for local logging and asynchronous transmission whichever radio is used.

\section{Driver-display design}
\label{sec:lit-display}

Any display, head-down or head-up, draws the driver's eyes or attention from the road. NHTSA's glance limits (Chapter~\ref{chap:intro}) treat a vehicle stopped at a light as being driven \cite{nhtsa2013}. A head-up position does not remove the problem: HUD use changes attention demand and driving performance \cite{liu2003}, and head-down assessment methods do not fully capture HUD behaviour \cite{smith2016}. A windshield HUD also brings optical problems: a double image from the two glass surfaces that wedge glass corrects only for one viewing geometry, an eye box that must be measured per installation, and legibility that depends on ambient light \cite{mareska2022, qin2017, hauslschmid2016}; misaligned or excessive graphics compete with hazards \cite{hauslschmid2015}. Each is harder on a removable, low-cost unit on a vibrating trike in daylight, and where a dashboard already carries speed and battery state a HUD adds a competing channel \cite{zhu2021}. These are the reasons the project used the existing dashboard.

For a dashboard, the literature gives five design issues against which a page can be checked. Layout and clutter: symbol count was the most influential interface factor in a semi-automated driving study, which recommended fewer than six symbols at a time \cite{park2020}, and added channels raise mental demand \cite{zhu2021}, so a stable hierarchy (persistent speed and battery, brief prioritised alerts, navigation only when needed) is preferred. Legibility and contrast: ISO~15008 sets character height by visual angle, 20 arc minutes recommended and 16 acceptable, as reported in \cite{reimer2014typeface, you2021charsize}; at about 70\,cm from a 10.1-inch, 1280 by 800 panel, 20 arc minutes is a capital height of about 24 pixels, and a humanist typeface cut total glance time by 10.6\% among male drivers \cite{reimer2014typeface}. Interaction: touch keys improved performance up to about 17.5\,mm \cite{kim2014touchkey}, Euro NCAP's 2026 protocol sets a minimum touch-target size \cite{euroncap2026engagement}, and entering a destination took about 40\,s on production systems \cite{strayer2017infotainment}, so such tasks belong to the parked state. Alert priority: safety alerts should be few, ordered and distinct from status. Data validity and update age: the guidance does not address stale data, but a display that keeps showing a value after its source has stopped presents an estimate as a measurement, so this project treats missing and out-of-date data as a requirement in its own right (R11). Evaluation should combine technical measures (render time, update rate, contrast in representative light, data age) with glance behaviour and workload in controlled trials.

\section{Cellular synchronisation and data integrity}
\label{sec:lit-sync}

The brief names MQTT and HTTP over 4G/LTE and asks for local logging and synchronisation with a central database \cite{winberg2026}. Since coverage and delay cannot be controlled, this project keeps the real-time path separate from synchronisation and measures delivery rather than assuming it: each record carries a timestamp, a session identifier and a sequence number so that gaps and duplicates can be detected, and the measures are completeness, backlog, delay, duplicates and recovery after an induced outage.

\section{Lightweight detection and monocular ranging}
\label{sec:lit-detection}

A camera alert on a vehicle without an accelerator needs a detector that runs on a general-purpose processor. SSD predicts classes and boxes for a fixed set of default boxes in one pass \cite{liu2016ssd}; MobileNet cuts the arithmetic of the feature extractor with depthwise separable convolutions \cite{howard2017mobilenets}; and integer-only quantisation runs such networks in 8-bit arithmetic at a small cost in accuracy \cite{jacob2018quantization}. A model trained on COCO knows only its classes, which include people, vehicles and animals but no road-surface hazards \cite{lin2014coco}. In a benchmark on edge devices including the Raspberry Pi~4, SSD MobileNet V1 had the lowest latency and energy and the lowest accuracy \cite{alqahtani2024edge}, the trade made here. Monocular distance rests on assumptions: inverse perspective mapping assumes a flat road and performs poorly for distant objects and on curves \cite{zhu2019distance}, and the width heuristic used here assumes a width per class, so it is indicative only and is fused with time-of-flight ranging. Whether an object is closing does not need the distance: at constant closing speed the time to contact equals the object's image size divided by its rate of growth \cite{lee1976tau}, and production single-camera forward-collision warning is built on this scale change \cite{dagan2004fcw}, which suits an uncalibrated camera. Objects outside the trained classes, such as debris, are a separate problem, approached with synthetic data \cite{choe2023hazardnet}, anomaly-segmentation benchmarks \cite{chan2021segmentmeifyoucan} and detectors trained on rare roadside obstacles \cite{tanveer2026yolov8n}.

\section{Proximity sensing on low-speed vehicles}
\label{sec:lit-proximity}

Nearby-object alerting is treated as a requirement even for slow vehicles: De Simone et al.\ classified ultrasonic ranges for obstacle avoidance on retrofitted agricultural machines \cite{desimone2018}, and Noomwongs et al.\ combined RTK-GNSS with a 2D LiDAR on a low-speed micro electric vehicle, because vision alone needed computing such vehicles lack \cite{noomwongs2025}. A multi-zone time-of-flight sensor such as the VL53L5CX resolves direction from one device; an independent characterisation found it near-linear against a white target but with a standard deviation near 50\,mm beyond 400\,mm and only 18.8 to 34.4\% valid readings against a black target, and a warm-up drift of about 15 minutes \cite{caroleo2026}. Range on dark targets and in sunlight therefore has to be measured on the vehicle, not taken from the datasheet.

\section{Platform options for acquisition and detection}
\label{sec:lit-platforms}

The reviewed systems use three classes of hardware, each suited to part of the workload. A microcontroller samples at a fixed period and processes short windows cheaply \cite{bui2026, alatoom2025}, but cannot run a camera network at a useful rate. A single-board computer such as the Raspberry Pi~4 can run a quantised detector \cite{jacob2018quantization, alqahtani2024edge}, but its Linux scheduler does not guarantee a fixed sampling period; P\'erez-Gonz\'alez et al.\ divide the work between the two \cite{perezgonzalez2025}. A neural accelerator beside the single-board computer would raise the frame rate at the cost of a further device to integrate; without one, vision needs computing that small vehicles may lack \cite{noomwongs2025}.

The literature therefore points to a split design: deterministic acquisition on a microcontroller, detection on a single-board computer, and an accelerator as an option to be measured rather than assumed. Table~\ref{tab:architectures} in Chapter~\ref{chap:design} compares these options with the figures measured in this project, and Section~\ref{sec:architecture} gives the choice made.

\section{Synthesis and research gap}
\label{sec:lit-synthesis}

The reviewed work shows that low-cost inertial sensors can detect road anomalies, that edge devices can process sensor windows locally, and that sparse, legible displays can be read within glance limits. It also shows three limitations: response-based roughness depends strongly on vehicle, speed, mounting and calibration; edge architectures are usually reported by accuracy or mean latency, without jitter, queue behaviour or outage recovery; and display guidance comes almost entirely from passenger cars.

The searches of Section~\ref{sec:lit-scope} found no system that integrates these concerns for a light electric cargo trike: fixed-rate dual-IMU acquisition with measured jitter and loss, on-board ride features, camera detection fused with time-of-flight ranging, LTE telemetry with local logging and replay, and presentation on the vehicle's own dashboard with missing data shown as absent. One reported system combining these elements on a light electric vehicle would have falsified the claim; Table~\ref{tab:gap} compares the closest systems cited here.

So a trike needs its own ride baseline, not a threshold from a car; a real-time telemetry system is judged by its worst cases and its recovery; and passenger-car display guidance has to be checked on the vehicle. The methods carried forward are:
\begin{itemize}
\item a vehicle-specific response index and event log, validated by repeatability over traversals and sensitivity to speed and payload, in place of an IRI (Section~\ref{sec:lit-roughness});
\item orientation compensation, windowed time-domain features, spectral features for narrow-band powertrain vibration, and speed-stratified comparison against stationary and coast-down baselines (Section~\ref{sec:lit-signal-processing});
\item a producer and consumer design with timestamped samples in bounded queues, with jitter, queue occupancy, display age and latency reported as distributions (Section~\ref{sec:lit-edge});
\item records with a timestamp, session identifier and sequence number, local logging, and measured completeness, backlog and recovery after an outage (Section~\ref{sec:lit-sync});
\item a sparse, stable dashboard hierarchy checked against glance limits, ISO~15008 character height and touch-target size, with missing and out-of-date data shown as such (Section~\ref{sec:lit-display});
\item a quantised single-pass detector, time to contact from image scale, and fusion with time-of-flight ranging measured on the vehicle (Sections~\ref{sec:lit-detection} and~\ref{sec:lit-proximity});
\item the split between deterministic acquisition on a microcontroller and detection on a single-board computer (Section~\ref{sec:lit-platforms}).
\end{itemize}
Chapter~\ref{chap:method} sets out how each of these was tested on the TUKZIE Rev 0, and Chapter~\ref{chap:design} how it was implemented.
