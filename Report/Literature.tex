\section{Scope and evaluation framework}
\label{sec:lit-scope}

The system developed in this project, a telemetry and hazard-warning unit for the TUKZIE Rev~0, draws on four fields that are usually studied apart: ride and road sensing, embedded signal processing, vehicular telemetry, and driver-display design. The literature is judged here by what the TUKZIE Rev 0 needs, not only by reported accuracy: computational cost, sampling requirements, sensitivity to speed and mounting, real-time latency, communication loss and driver workload.

The review follows these fields from the case for measuring a cargo trike to the methods this project adopts, and ends by testing whether any existing system already combines them. To keep it relevant and current, an automated search of OpenAlex, arXiv and Crossref was run on a fixed query set (ride characterisation, three-wheeler dynamics, cellular vehicle telemetry, MQTT, IMU calibration and fusion, camera hazard detection on edge hardware, driver displays and BMS protocols), in one session on 19 September 2026, in three passes as the query set grew from 2 to 21 queries, giving 733 records (\texttt{Research/research.db}). Manual searches of Google Scholar, IEEE Xplore and the UCT Libraries throughout the project, sources from the supervisor and the brief, and a targeted search of the three-wheeler market and open-source projects added work the databases miss; the papers used are filed by topic in \texttt{References/Papers}. The gap claim of Section~\ref{sec:lit-synthesis} is bounded by these searches.

Section~\ref{sec:lit-trikes} sets out why an electric cargo trike needs on-vehicle measurement. Sections~\ref{sec:lit-roughness} to~\ref{sec:lit-proximity} review ride response, inertial signal processing, the calibration, timing and position on which every measurement rests, edge architecture, driver displays, cellular synchronisation, camera detection and proximity sensing, and Section~\ref{sec:lit-platforms} compares hardware platforms. Section~\ref{sec:lit-synthesis} states the gap and the methods carried forward. Figure~\ref{fig:lit-map} shows which research question each theme informs.

\section{Electric cargo trikes and the case for telemetry}
\label{sec:lit-trikes}

How much an electric freight vehicle saves is not a fixed number: it depends on the route, the load, energy and fuel prices, and what the vehicle replaces. The two studies closest to the TUKZIE both worked from vehicles in real service. Sebwa et al.\ assessed an electric tricycle on medium-size cargo deliveries in Dar es Salaam and found it cost a little over half as much to run as a motorcycle and about a seventh as much as a light car (45.5 and 86\% less) \cite{sebwa2025}; Saenz et al.\ found that a tricycle delivery service in Portland cut its carbon footprint by 51 to 72\% \cite{saenz2016}. Neither result carries over to another city by itself. An operator can only check the saving for its own fleet by logging the same energy, distance and use on its own vehicles, which is the job of on-vehicle telemetry. A three-wheeled chassis also makes vehicle response an engineering concern: payload position, tyre pressure, speed and surface affect both stability and measured vibration, so operating state should be recorded with the inertial data, and passenger-car roughness thresholds should not be transferred directly.

\section{Road roughness and ride response}
\label{sec:lit-roughness}

The International Roughness Index (IRI) is computed from a measured road profile through a standard quarter-car model \cite{fhwa2016}. An accelerometer on a vehicle measures something else: the combined response of road, tyres, suspension, chassis, payload, speed and mounting, with the powertrain's own vibration added (Figure~\ref{fig:lit-chain}). Gonz\'alez et al.\ classified road profiles from simulated vehicle accelerations \cite{gonzalez2008}, but the inference depends on the assumed vehicle model, which cannot be carried over to a trike. Smartphone and low-cost studies extend the response-based approach, and identify placement, orientation, speed, sampling rate and inconsistent ground truth as persistent sources of variation \cite{sattar2018}; Alatoom and Smadi's microcontroller and single-board-computer framework is closer to this project because it allows controlled mounting and deterministic sampling \cite{alatoom2025}. None of these establishes a calibrated ride measure for a three-wheeled cargo vehicle. The defensible first claim for this project is therefore a repeatable, vehicle-specific response index and event log, validated by repeatability over traversals and sensitivity to speed and payload, not an IRI.

\section{Inertial signal processing and vibration separation}
\label{sec:lit-signal-processing}

An inertial signal contains gravity, orientation changes, road excitation, body motion, drivetrain vibration, noise and transients. Features from a nominally vertical axis are comparable only after orientation compensation, and become unreliable when samples are irregular or missing. Time-domain features (root-mean-square, standard deviation, peak-to-peak, crest factor, jerk, threshold counts) are cheap and interpretable; spectral energy and power spectral density separate sustained narrow-band vibration from broadband impacts. Bui et al.\ show rough-road detection running on an edge microcontroller \cite{bui2026}. Yiliguoqi et al.\ classified roughness with an SVM on vertical acceleration and roll features at only 10\,Hz, with per-class discrimination rates of 80 to 100\% but an accuracy of only 70 to 80\% when a model trained on one of their vehicles was tested on another (85\% when trained on three) \cite{yiliguoqi2026}; that range is tied to their phones, routes, labels and thresholds and cannot be expected on the TUKZIE without retraining. Learned classifiers should therefore follow a transparent statistical baseline, not replace calibration.

Separating road excitation from powertrain vibration needs an empirical baseline: stationary runs at several motor speeds identify repeatable powertrain components, and coast-down and repeated route runs show which components remain without traction and which change with the surface. Windowed statistics should be normalised or stratified by speed, because the same road feature excites the vehicle at a different frequency at a different speed \cite{li2018}. How the response scales with speed is itself vehicle-specific: Li et al.\ divided mean-square acceleration by speed \cite{li2018}, whereas SmartRoadSense, aggregating smartphone data from many cars, found its roughness index locally followed a power law in speed \cite{alessandroni2017}. Dividing by speed assumes an exponent of one, which should be fitted for the trike. ISO~2631-1 defines the evaluation of whole-body vibration up to 80\,Hz \cite{iso2631}; its weighted measures describe comfort, which should be reported separately from vehicle response and road geometry.

\section{Calibration, timing and position}
\label{sec:lit-calibration}

Every inertial feature also rests on the sensor's scale, the time of each sample and the vehicle's position and speed; each has an established method and a known limit.

\emph{Inertial calibration.} The deterministic errors of a MEMS accelerometer are dominated by bias, scale factor, non-orthogonality and misalignment, and its offset drifts with temperature and age \cite{ru2022}. The classic laboratory method is the static six-position test: each axis is pointed up and then down against gravity, and the half-sum and half-difference of the two readings give its bias and scale factor; its accuracy depends on how well the axes are aligned with the vertical, which usually needs a turntable or a precision cube \cite{ru2022}. Autonomous methods drop the fixture, using only the fact that the resting magnitude equals local gravity. Tedaldi et al.\ estimated scale factors, axis misalignments and biases from a set of static positions reached by moving the sensor by hand \cite{tedaldi2014}; Webering et al.\ found 12 orientations sufficient where Tedaldi et al.\ had recommended 37 to 50, cutting a calibration from about 5 to under 2 minutes \cite{webering2022}. One factor per sensor that scales the resting magnitude to gravity, as used here (Section~\ref{sec:imu-magnitude-calibration}), is the one-orientation case of this model: it removes the gain difference between two sensors but leaves the per-axis biases, so it holds only in the orientation in which it was taken. Vibration is a further reason to recalibrate. De Pasquale and Som\`a repeated a six-position calibration on a commercial MEMS unit before and after accelerated vibration and treated changes in bias, scale factor and non-orthogonality between axes as signs of internal damage \cite{depasquale2010}. On a trike the defensible practice is a calibration in the mounted position, repeated and compared over time, with a multi-orientation calibration as the upgrade if axis-resolved features are needed.

\emph{Clock synchronisation.} NTP, which chrony implements, estimates a clock's offset from round-trip exchanges, and its specification gives a few hundred microseconds as typical for clients on a fast local network \cite{rfc5905}, ample against a 5\,ms sample period. Elson et al.\ removed the sender's non-determinism from the critical path by letting receivers timestamp a common broadcast, reaching 1.85 $\pm$ 1.28\,$\mu$s agreement over 802.11 \cite{elson2002}. Olson addressed sensors that cannot take part in any protocol: timestamping data on arrival at a host without a real-time operating system adds jitter that can reach hundreds of milliseconds under load, and fitting the sensor's clock to the host's using the lowest-latency messages reduced the error \cite{olson2010}. So arrival times on a Raspberry Pi are not sample times: each node should timestamp its own samples and map them to the host clock through a shared physical event, a reference broadcast carried on a wire. And since a protocol's error bound describes the network path, not the delay from a sensor to its timestamp, the residual offset between modalities has to be measured with a common stimulus.

\emph{Positioning.} Merry and Bettinger measured an average horizontal error of 7 to 13\,m for a smartphone in an urban environment, in line with recreation-grade receivers where multipath is likely \cite{merry2019}. A phone is therefore not a ground truth at the 10\,m scale: agreement between the modem and a phone is a consistency check of two similar errors, and an absolute figure needs a surveyed point or a higher-grade receiver. GNSS speed, which normalises the ride index, likewise needs an independent check.

For the research questions this means that R2, R4 and R6 can at best show consistency (a stable factor, agreement with a phone, a regular pulse), and that RQ1's accuracy claim depends on the residual offsets and drifts being measured on the vehicle.

\section{Edge computing and real-time telemetry architecture}
\label{sec:lit-edge}

Edge computing moves processing close to the sensors to reduce response time, bandwidth and dependence on a remote service \cite{shi2016}, which suits a vehicle whose alerts must work without coverage and whose raw 200\,Hz data would cost more to transmit than compact features; embedded HPEC also lets several streams be timestamped and fused on the vehicle \cite{barnell2018}. ``High performance'' here means predictable completion of time-critical work within a small power budget, not throughput. Threads alone do not give real-time behaviour: shared locks, blocking input and output, allocation and unbounded queues still cause missed samples or stale displays. The appropriate pattern is a producer and consumer design with timestamped samples in bounded queues, explicit overload behaviour (an old display frame may be dropped, an inertial sample must not be lost silently), and jitter, queue occupancy, display age and latency reported as distributions.

The closest comparison is an electric-racing telemetry system with ESP32 acquisition, Raspberry Pi~4B analytics and LoRaWAN, reporting 99\% packet delivery and sub-second latency on its track \cite{perezgonzalez2025}. Its division of acquisition from processing supports the architecture used here; its radio result is specific to its gateway and route. LTE gives wider reach at the cost of subscription, coverage dependence and less predictable delay, which argues for local logging and asynchronous transmission whichever radio is used.

\section{Driver-display design}
\label{sec:lit-display}

Any display, head-down or head-up, draws the driver's eyes or attention from the road. NHTSA's glance limits (Chapter~\ref{chap:intro}) treat a vehicle stopped at a light as being driven \cite{nhtsa2013}. A head-up position does not remove the problem: HUD use changes attention demand and driving performance \cite{liu2003}, and head-down assessment methods do not fully capture HUD behaviour \cite{smith2016}. A windshield HUD also brings optical problems: a double image from the two glass surfaces that wedge glass corrects only for one viewing geometry, an eye box that must be measured per installation, and legibility that depends on ambient light \cite{mareska2022, qin2017, hauslschmid2016}; misaligned or excessive graphics compete with hazards \cite{hauslschmid2015}. Each is harder on a removable, low-cost unit on a vibrating trike in daylight, and where a dashboard already carries speed and battery state a HUD adds a competing channel \cite{zhu2021}. These are the reasons the project used the existing dashboard.

For a dashboard, the literature gives five design issues against which a page can be checked. Layout and clutter: symbol count was the most influential interface factor in a semi-automated driving study, which recommended fewer than six symbols at a time \cite{park2020}, and added channels raise mental demand \cite{zhu2021}, so a stable hierarchy (persistent speed and battery, brief prioritised alerts, navigation only when needed) is preferred. Legibility and contrast: ISO~15008 sets the capital height by visual angle at the driver's eye, 20 arc minutes recommended, 16 acceptable and 12 the minimum where reading is undemanding \cite{iso15008}; at about 70\,cm from a 10.1-inch, 1280 by 800 panel, 20 arc minutes is a capital height of about 24 pixels, and a humanist typeface cut total glance time by 10.6\% among male drivers \cite{reimer2014typeface}. Interaction: touch keys improved performance up to about 17.5\,mm \cite{kim2014touchkey}, Euro NCAP's 2026 protocol sets a minimum touch-target size \cite{euroncap2026engagement}, and entering a destination took about 40\,s on production systems \cite{strayer2017infotainment}, so such tasks belong to the parked state. Alert priority: safety alerts should be few, ordered and distinct from status. Data validity and update age: the guidance does not address stale data, but a display that keeps showing a value after its source has stopped presents an estimate as a measurement, so this project treats missing and out-of-date data as a requirement in its own right (R11). Evaluation should combine technical measures (render time, update rate, contrast in representative light, data age) with glance behaviour and workload in controlled trials.

\section{Cellular synchronisation and data integrity}
\label{sec:lit-sync}

The brief names MQTT and HTTP over 4G/LTE and asks for local logging and synchronisation with a central database \cite{winberg2026}. MQTT defines three delivery levels: at most once (QoS~0); at least once, where messages arrive but duplicates can occur (QoS~1); and exactly once (QoS~2) \cite{mqtt311}. The guarantee covers only messages already handed to the client, and only while the session state survives; the standard notes that this state may be discarded, or lost through hardware or software failure \cite{mqtt311}. A record produced while the modem has no coverage, or held in memory when the vehicle is switched off, is outside it. QoS~2 removes duplicates at the cost of a four-packet exchange per message; QoS~1 with a unique key per record and deduplication at the database reaches the same end state with half the exchanges over a slow link.

For networks with frequent partitions, Fall proposed asynchronous messages kept in persistent storage and retransmitted hop by hop, with responsibility passed on by acknowledged custody transfer so that a resource-poor end node need not wait for an end-to-end acknowledgement \cite{fall2003}. A telemetry unit is a one-hop case: the flash log is the persistent store, the broker's acknowledgement is the custody transfer, and a ledger of acknowledged sequence numbers decides what to replay after a reboot or an outage. Chapungo and Postolache tested LoRaWAN at a rural Mozambican site with neither electricity nor a mobile network \cite{chapungo2025}, and Ugwuanyi et al.\ identified coverage and power as limits on IoT deployment in developing countries \cite{ugwuanyi2021}. An urban route in Cape Town is better served, and neither study measured cellular delivery from a moving vehicle, so their figures do not transfer; what transfers is that coverage is a variable to be recorded, not a premise.

Since coverage and delay cannot be controlled, this project keeps the real-time path separate from synchronisation and measures delivery rather than assuming it: each record carries a timestamp, a session identifier and a sequence number so that gaps and duplicates can be detected, and the measures are completeness, backlog, delay, duplicates and recovery after an induced outage. The telemetry systems reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:lit-edge} report delivery ratio and mean latency; recovery after an outage is the measure this project adds for RQ3.

\section{Lightweight detection and monocular ranging}
\label{sec:lit-detection}

A camera alert on a vehicle without an accelerator needs a detector that runs on a general-purpose processor. SSD predicts classes and boxes for a fixed set of default boxes in one pass \cite{liu2016ssd}; MobileNet cuts the arithmetic of the feature extractor with depthwise separable convolutions \cite{howard2017mobilenets}; and integer-only quantisation runs such networks in 8-bit arithmetic at a small cost in accuracy \cite{jacob2018quantization}. A model trained on COCO knows only its classes, which include people, vehicles and animals but no road-surface hazards \cite{lin2014coco}. In a benchmark on edge devices including the Raspberry Pi~4, SSD MobileNet V1 had the lowest latency and energy and the lowest accuracy \cite{alqahtani2024edge}, the trade made here. Monocular distance rests on assumptions: inverse perspective mapping assumes a flat road and performs poorly for distant objects and on curves \cite{zhu2019distance}, and the width heuristic used here assumes a width per class, so it is indicative only and is fused with time-of-flight ranging. Whether an object is closing does not need the distance: at constant closing speed the time to contact equals the object's image size divided by its rate of growth \cite{lee1976tau}, and production single-camera forward-collision warning is built on this scale change \cite{dagan2004fcw}, which suits an uncalibrated camera. Objects outside the trained classes, such as debris, are a separate problem, approached with synthetic data \cite{choe2023hazardnet}, anomaly-segmentation benchmarks \cite{chan2021segmentmeifyoucan} and detectors trained on rare roadside obstacles \cite{tanveer2026yolov8n}.

Validating a time-to-contact warning needs an independent measure of the true time to contact. Manglik et al.\ obtained ground truth for a monocular forecaster from a LiDAR accurate to a few centimetres, calibrated to the camera, and reported an average error of 0.75\,s for people approaching a pushed platform at 0.2 to 1.5\,m/s; they cautioned that the model might not generalise to other camera heights, speeds or scenes \cite{manglik2019}. For production forward-collision warning, NHTSA's confirmation test drove a car at 72.4\,km/h towards a stopped, a braking or a slower sedan and required the warning at a time to collision of at least 2.1, 2.4 and 2.0\,s respectively, in at least five of seven trials \cite{lenkeit2012fcw}. Neither transfers directly: the trike's top speed is 42\,km/h, its likely targets are pedestrians, carts and light vehicles rather than a sedan, and an error of 0.75\,s is large against a 2\,s threshold. What transfers is the form of the test: an independent distance reference on a clock common with the camera's, a pass criterion fixed before the run, and repeated trials, including objects that are still or receding and should raise no warning. Test C11 takes this form but has not yet been run.

\section{Proximity sensing on low-speed vehicles}
\label{sec:lit-proximity}

Nearby-object alerting is treated as a requirement even for slow vehicles: De Simone et al.\ classified ultrasonic ranges for obstacle avoidance on retrofitted agricultural machines \cite{desimone2018}, and Noomwongs et al.\ combined RTK-GNSS with a 2D LiDAR on a low-speed micro electric vehicle, because vision alone needed computing such vehicles lack \cite{noomwongs2025}. A multi-zone time-of-flight sensor such as the VL53L5CX resolves direction from one device; an independent characterisation found it near-linear against a white target but with a standard deviation near 50\,mm beyond 400\,mm and only 18.8 to 34.4\% valid readings against a black target, and a warm-up drift of about 15 minutes \cite{caroleo2026}. Range on dark targets and in sunlight therefore has to be measured on the vehicle, not taken from the datasheet.

For combining such a sensor with a camera, Yeong et al.\ distinguished high-level fusion, in which each sensor detects independently and the results are then combined, from low-level fusion of raw data: the former is simpler and is often adopted for that reason, but discards information such as low-confidence detections where obstacles overlap, while the latter needs precise extrinsic and temporal calibration \cite{yeong2021}. Matching a camera detection to a time-of-flight cone by bearing is high-level fusion: cheap enough for a single-board computer and free of pixel-level calibration, but correct only if the sensors' bearings are known and their readings current at the frame's time, both of which have to be measured (Section~\ref{sec:lit-calibration}). Reviewing near-field perception for low-speed automation of cars, Eising et al.\ noted that arrays of ultrasonic sensors are a mature, low-cost source of near-field safety and argued for redundancy between sensor types \cite{eising2022}. On a trike a range sensor beside the camera offers that redundancy at shorter range, not better classification.

\section{Platform options for acquisition and detection}
\label{sec:lit-platforms}

The reviewed systems use three classes of hardware, each suited to part of the workload. A microcontroller samples at a fixed period and processes short windows cheaply \cite{bui2026, alatoom2025}, but cannot run a camera network at a useful rate. A single-board computer such as the Raspberry Pi~4 can run a quantised detector \cite{jacob2018quantization, alqahtani2024edge}, but its Linux scheduler does not guarantee a fixed sampling period; P\'erez-Gonz\'alez et al.\ divide the work between the two \cite{perezgonzalez2025}. A neural accelerator beside the single-board computer would raise the frame rate at the cost of a further device to integrate; without one, vision needs computing that small vehicles may lack \cite{noomwongs2025}.

The literature therefore points to a split design: deterministic acquisition on a microcontroller, detection on a single-board computer, and an accelerator as an option to be measured rather than assumed. Table~\ref{tab:architectures} in Chapter~\ref{chap:design} compares these options with the figures measured in this project, and Section~\ref{sec:architecture} gives the choice made.

\section{Synthesis and research gap}
\label{sec:lit-synthesis}

The reviewed work shows that low-cost inertial sensors can detect road anomalies, that edge devices can process sensor windows locally, and that sparse, legible displays can be read within glance limits. It also shows four limitations: response-based roughness depends strongly on vehicle, speed, mounting and calibration; edge architectures are usually reported by accuracy or mean latency, without jitter, queue behaviour or outage recovery; display guidance comes almost entirely from passenger cars; and the validation methods for calibration, timing and warnings were developed on laboratory platforms or cars at speeds and with targets unlike a trike's.

The searches of Section~\ref{sec:lit-scope} found no system that integrates these concerns for a light electric cargo trike: fixed-rate dual-IMU acquisition with measured jitter and loss, on-board ride features, camera detection fused with time-of-flight ranging, LTE telemetry with local logging and replay, and presentation on the vehicle's own dashboard with missing data shown as absent. One reported system combining these elements on a light electric vehicle would have falsified the claim; Table~\ref{tab:gap} compares the closest systems cited here.

So a trike needs its own ride baseline, not a threshold from a car; a real-time telemetry system is judged by its worst cases and its recovery; and passenger-car display guidance has to be checked on the vehicle. The methods carried forward are:
\begin{itemize}
\item a vehicle-specific response index and event log, validated by repeatability over traversals and sensitivity to speed and payload, in place of an IRI (Section~\ref{sec:lit-roughness});
\item orientation compensation, windowed time-domain features, spectral features for narrow-band powertrain vibration, and speed-stratified comparison against stationary and coast-down baselines (Section~\ref{sec:lit-signal-processing});
\item a calibration taken in the mounted position and repeated over time, node timestamps mapped to one host clock through a shared physical event with the residual offset measured, and GNSS speed checked against an independent reference, a phone being only a consistency check for position (Section~\ref{sec:lit-calibration});
\item a producer and consumer design with timestamped samples in bounded queues, with jitter, queue occupancy, display age and latency reported as distributions (Section~\ref{sec:lit-edge});
\item records with a timestamp, session identifier and sequence number, published at least once and deduplicated by key, a persistent local log as the store for replay, and measured completeness, backlog and recovery after an outage (Section~\ref{sec:lit-sync});
\item a sparse, stable dashboard hierarchy checked against glance limits, ISO~15008 character height and touch-target size, with missing and out-of-date data shown as such (Section~\ref{sec:lit-display});
\item a quantised single-pass detector, time to contact from image scale validated against an independent distance reference with criteria fixed beforehand, and high-level fusion with time-of-flight ranging measured on the vehicle (Sections~\ref{sec:lit-detection} and~\ref{sec:lit-proximity});
\item the split between deterministic acquisition on a microcontroller and detection on a single-board computer (Section~\ref{sec:lit-platforms}).
\end{itemize}
