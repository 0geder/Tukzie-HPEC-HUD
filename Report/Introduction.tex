\section{The vehicle and the problem}

The TUKZIE Flagship Programme is developing an energy-aware, secure and autonomy-ready platform for last-mile logistics and urban freight, a market growing with cities \cite{unwup2019}. Its testbed is the TUKZIE Rev 0, a 72\,V electric cargo trike made by Jinpeng \cite{jinpengmanual}. Its nameplates record a three-phase, permanent-magnet, sensored hub motor (72\,V DC, 35\,A, 90\,A controller limit, about 2.5\,kW rated input) \cite{adamsrasara2026}, and the vehicle's plate (Figure~\ref{fig:vehicle-nameplate}) gives model L2e-U, 3.9\,kW rated output and 42\,km/h. Such tricycles already carry urban goods in African cities more cheaply than motorcycles and light cars (Section~\ref{sec:lit-trikes}).

At the start the vehicle's touchscreen dashboard on a Raspberry Pi~5, written by earlier vacation-work teams, showed only simulated values (Section~\ref{sec:dashboard-link}); nothing measured the trike's response to the road and powertrain, the battery was not read, and no journey was recorded. A vehicle meant for fleet use and later autonomy needs all of these. Road traffic injuries are a leading cause of death, mostly in low- and middle-income countries \cite{who2023}, so hazard alerts are worth having if the display does not itself distract: NHTSA guidance limits single glances away from the road to 2\,s and a task to 12\,s \cite{nhtsa2013}.

The engineering problem is to acquire synchronised data reliably on a high-vibration, power-constrained vehicle; to distinguish road-induced motion from vehicle-specific dynamics; to keep latency bounded; to preserve telemetry through intermittent connectivity; and to present the result without obstructing the driver's view or depending on a remote service \cite{winberg2026}. The objective of this project is to develop a high-performance embedded computing telemetry unit for the TUKZIE Rev~0, integrated with the vehicle's existing dashboard, that acquires synchronised inertial, battery, positioning and camera data, characterises the vehicle's dynamic response at the edge, preserves telemetry through intermittent cellular connectivity, and presents a minimal set of safety-relevant information to the driver on that dashboard.

\section{Deliverables and scope change}

The brief \cite{winberg2026} asked for (1) a high-performance embedded computing (HPEC) telemetry unit with a deterministic multi-threaded architecture for sensor polling, signal processing, display rendering and networking; (2) a driver interface, originally a windshield head-up display (HUD); (3) synchronisation with a central database over 4G/LTE; and (4) a technical report validating the data and the system. With the supervisor's agreement (1 October 2026, title change recorded 2 October) the HUD was replaced by integration with the existing dashboard, which already carries what a HUD would show; a second display would compete for the same attention \cite{zhu2021} (Section~\ref{sec:lit-display}). On 23 October, with the same agreement and the convenor's, the title was changed again to name the road characterisation and hazard detection the work had grown to include. A camera hazard detector was added on the supervisor's direction, then time-of-flight proximity sensing, and in October a second camera and sensor board at the rear, with a warning for closing objects. The intended outcome is a prototype demonstrated on the vehicle, Technology Readiness Level 6 \cite{kimmel2020}, not a product for unrestricted road use.

\section{Research questions}

\begin{enumerate}
\item[RQ1] How accurately and repeatably can a low-cost edge-processing system characterise the trike's dynamic response to road- and powertrain-induced excitation in real time, using inertial sensors on a 72\,V electric cargo trike?
\item[RQ2] Which information hierarchy, layout and update strategy can present safety-critical telemetry and hazard alerts on the vehicle's existing dashboard while limiting visual clutter and driver distraction?
\item[RQ3] What end-to-end latency, data-loss and recovery performance can be achieved across sensor acquisition, edge processing, local logging and 4G/LTE synchronisation?
\item[RQ4] Which time- and frequency-domain features most effectively separate road-induced vibration from repeatable motor and powertrain vibration?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope and limitations}

Delivered, tested on the bench and run on the vehicle: the ESP32-S3 telemetry unit (IMU, GNSS, battery-management system over Bluetooth Low Energy, LTE, local logging, replay into a database) and, from 22 October, a front IMU node; forward and rear camera units with a pretrained detector and three fused time-of-flight sensors each; and their integration with the existing dashboard. In scope but not delivered, and so future work (Chapter~\ref{chap:recs}): the SEN55 air-quality sensor, which came without its cable and was damaged while wires were soldered to its test pads, so no environmental data were recorded (Section~\ref{sec:environmental-sensing}); the VL53L5CX time-of-flight sensor, not received (three VL53L0X sensors stand in); the motor Hall tap; and LTE publishing from the moving vehicle. The windshield HUD is not built. The limitations are one vehicle and one set of hardware; a pretrained, general-purpose detector that stores no frames, identifies no individual and controls nothing; calibration under bench conditions; vehicle testing in short, low-speed sessions; and results mostly from the bench.
