\section{The vehicle and the problem}

The TUKZIE Flagship Programme is developing an energy-aware, secure and autonomy-ready platform for last-mile logistics and urban freight, a market that is growing as cities grow \cite{unwup2019}. Its testbed is the TUKZIE Rev 0, a 72\,V electric cargo trike manufactured by Jinpeng \cite{jinpengmanual}. Inspection of the motor and controller nameplates recorded a three-phase, permanent-magnet, sensored hub motor rated at 72\,V DC and 35\,A with a controller current limit of 90\,A, about 2.5\,kW of rated input power \cite{adamsrasara2026}; the vehicle's own plate (Figure~\ref{fig:vehicle-nameplate}, Appendix~\ref{app:figures}) gives model L2e-U, a rated output of 3.9\,kW and a rated speed of 42\,km/h. Electric tricycles of this kind already carry urban goods in African cities, where one study found operating costs per kilometre 45.5 to 86\% below those of motorcycles, petrol tuk-tuks and a light car on the routes it measured \cite{sebwa2025}.

At the start of this project the vehicle had a touchscreen dashboard on a Raspberry Pi~5, written by earlier vacation-work teams, but no live data: every page was driven by simulated values (Section~\ref{sec:dashboard-link}). Nothing measured how the trike responds to the road and its own powertrain, the battery state was not read, and there was no record of a journey. A vehicle intended for fleet use and later autonomy needs all of these, and it needs them on a platform that vibrates strongly, has limited power and computing, and loses cellular coverage. Road traffic injuries are also a leading cause of death, mostly in low- and middle-income countries \cite{who2023}, so hazard alerts for the driver are worth having, provided that the display does not itself become a distraction: NHTSA guidance limits single glances away from the road to 2\,s and a task to 12\,s in total \cite{nhtsa2013}.

The engineering problem is therefore to acquire synchronised data reliably on a high-vibration, power-constrained vehicle; to distinguish road-induced motion from vehicle-specific dynamics; to keep processing and display latency bounded; to preserve telemetry through intermittent connectivity; and to present the result to the driver without obstructing the view or making safety-relevant behaviour depend on a remote service \cite{winberg2026}.

\section{Deliverables and scope change}

The brief \cite{winberg2026} asked for: (1) an embedded high-performance embedded computing (HPEC) telemetry unit with a deterministic multi-threaded architecture for sensor polling, signal processing, display rendering and networking; (2) a driver interface, originally a windshield head-up display (HUD); (3) synchronisation of telemetry with a central database over 4G/LTE; and (4) a technical report validating the data and the system. During the project the windshield HUD was replaced, with the supervisor's agreement on 1 October 2026 and a title change recorded by the course coordinator on 2 October, by integration with the vehicle's existing dashboard: that dashboard already carries the information a HUD would show, a second display would compete for the same attention \cite{zhu2021}, and the camera's hazard alerts serve the programme's move towards autonomy better than a second screen (Section~\ref{sec:lit-display}). The camera hazard detector was added to the scope on the supervisor's direction, and time-of-flight proximity sensing after a later request for a live nearby-object alert.

The intended outcome was an integrated prototype demonstrated on the vehicle, Technology Readiness Level 6 \cite{kimmel2020}, not a product qualified for unrestricted road use.

\section{Research questions}

\begin{enumerate}
\item[RQ1] How accurately and repeatably can a low-cost edge-processing system characterise the trike's dynamic response to road- and powertrain-induced excitation in real time, using inertial sensors on a 72\,V electric cargo trike?
\item[RQ2] Which information hierarchy, layout and update strategy can present safety-critical telemetry and hazard alerts on the vehicle's existing dashboard while limiting visual clutter and driver distraction?
\item[RQ3] What end-to-end latency, data-loss and recovery performance can be achieved across sensor acquisition, edge processing, local logging and 4G/LTE synchronisation?
\item[RQ4] Which time- and frequency-domain features most effectively separate road-induced vibration from repeatable motor and powertrain vibration?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope and limitations}

In scope and delivered on the bench: the ESP32-S3 telemetry unit (two IMUs, GNSS, battery-management system over Bluetooth Low Energy, LTE, local logging); a forward camera on a Raspberry Pi~4 with a pretrained object detector, time-synchronised to the inertial stream; three time-of-flight proximity sensors fused with the camera detections; and the integration of all of these with the existing PySide6 dashboard on the Raspberry Pi~5. In scope but not delivered by the time of writing: the Sensirion SEN55 air-quality sensor (received, cable outstanding, Section~\ref{sec:environmental-sensing}); the 8$\times$8-zone VL53L5CX time-of-flight sensor (three VL53L0X sensors stand in); the motor Hall tap; and synchronisation with a central database (data reach only a public test broker). The windshield HUD is not built.

The limitations are: one vehicle and one set of hardware; a pretrained, general-purpose detector that reports object class, a coarse distance band, confidence, timestamp and alert status only, stores no frames, identifies no individual, does not track across frames and controls no vehicle function; calibration under bench conditions, without the effect of temperature or humidity being measured; vehicle testing limited by time to urban routes, without rural or off-road conditions; and results that are, unless stated otherwise, from the bench.
