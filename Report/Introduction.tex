\section{The vehicle and the problem}

The TUKZIE Flagship Programme of the University of Cape Town \cite{tukzie2026web} is developing an energy-aware, secure and autonomy-ready platform \cite{winberg2026} for last-mile logistics in growing cities \cite{unwup2019}. Its testbed is the TUKZIE Rev 0, a 72\,V electric cargo trike made by Jinpeng \cite{jinpengmanual}. Its sensored hub motor is rated at about 2.5\,kW input at 72\,V \cite{adamsrasara2026}; the vehicle's plate (Figure~\ref{fig:vehicle-nameplate}) gives 3.9\,kW output and 42\,km/h, more output than that input, so one plate is wrong; the motor's power was not measured. Such tricycles already carry urban goods in African cities more cheaply than motorcycles and light cars (Section~\ref{sec:lit-trikes}).

At the start the vehicle's touchscreen dashboard on a Raspberry Pi~5 showed only simulated values (Section~\ref{sec:dashboard-link}); nothing measured the trike's response to the road and powertrain, the battery was not read, and no journey was recorded. Road traffic injuries are a leading cause of death, mostly in low- and middle-income countries \cite{who2023}, so hazard alerts are worth having if the display does not itself distract: NHTSA guidance limits single glances away from the road to 2\,s and a task to 12\,s \cite{nhtsa2013}.

The engineering problem is to acquire synchronised data reliably on a high-vibration, power-constrained vehicle; to distinguish road-induced motion from vehicle-specific dynamics; to keep latency bounded; to preserve telemetry through intermittent connectivity; and to present the result without obstructing the driver's view or depending on a remote service \cite{winberg2026}. The objective is an HPEC telemetry unit for the TUKZIE Rev~0, integrated with its existing dashboard, that acquires synchronised inertial, battery, positioning and camera data, characterises the vehicle's response at the edge, keeps telemetry through intermittent coverage and shows the driver a minimal set of safety information.

\section{Deliverables and scope change}

The brief \cite{winberg2026} asked for (1) a high-performance embedded computing (HPEC) telemetry unit with a deterministic multi-threaded architecture; (2) a driver interface, originally a windshield head-up display (HUD); (3) synchronisation with a central database over 4G/LTE; and (4) a technical report validating the data and the system. With the supervisor's agreement the HUD was replaced by integration with the existing dashboard, because a second display would compete for the same attention \cite{zhu2021} (Section~\ref{sec:lit-display}), and the title was changed on 2 and 23 October to follow the work. A camera hazard detector was added on the supervisor's direction, then time-of-flight proximity sensing, and in October a second camera and sensor board at the rear, with a warning for closing objects. The intended outcome is a prototype demonstrated on the vehicle, Technology Readiness Level 6 \cite{kimmel2020}, not a product for unrestricted road use.

\section{Research questions}

The literature search found no system that combines, on a light electric cargo trike, fixed-rate inertial acquisition with measured timing and loss, on-board ride features, camera and range fusion, cellular telemetry with local logging and replay, and display on the vehicle's own dashboard (Section~\ref{sec:lit-synthesis}, Table~\ref{tab:gap}). The contribution sought is that integration and its measurement, not any single component:

\begin{enumerate}
\item[RQ1] How accurately and repeatably can a low-cost edge-processing system characterise the trike's dynamic response to road- and powertrain-induced excitation in real time, using inertial sensors on a 72\,V electric cargo trike?
\item[RQ2] Which information hierarchy, layout and update strategy can present safety-critical telemetry and hazard alerts on the vehicle's existing dashboard while limiting visual clutter and driver distraction?
\item[RQ3] What end-to-end latency, data-loss and recovery performance can be achieved across sensor acquisition, edge processing, local logging and 4G/LTE synchronisation?
\item[RQ4] Which time- and frequency-domain features most effectively separate road-induced vibration from repeatable motor and powertrain vibration?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope, limitations and plan of development}

Delivered, tested on the bench and run on the vehicle (GNSS and LTE publishing on the bench only): the ESP32-S3 telemetry unit (IMU, GNSS, battery-management system over Bluetooth Low Energy, LTE, local logging, replay into a database) and, from 22 October, a front IMU node; forward and rear camera units with a pretrained detector and three fused time-of-flight sensors each; and their integration with the existing dashboard. In scope but not delivered, and so future work (Chapter~\ref{chap:recs}): the SEN55 air-quality sensor, damaged in wiring (Section~\ref{sec:environmental-sensing}); the VL53L5CX time-of-flight sensor, not received; the motor Hall tap; and LTE publishing from the moving vehicle. Road-surface characterisation here means the vehicle's ride response and bump events, not a road profile (Section~\ref{sec:lit-roughness}). The limitations are one vehicle and one set of hardware, a pretrained detector that controls nothing, bench calibration, short low-speed vehicle sessions and results mostly from the bench. Chapters~\ref{chap:lit} to~\ref{chap:recs} follow in the order above, with detail in the appendices.
