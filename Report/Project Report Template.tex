{\Large Terms of Reference}\\
\hrule

This project (SW-7) is to develop a high-performance embedded computing (HPEC) telemetry unit for the TUKZIE Rev 0 electric cargo trike, integrated with the vehicle's existing dashboard, capable of acquiring synchronised inertial, battery, positioning and camera data, characterising the vehicle's dynamic response at the edge, preserving telemetry under intermittent cellular connectivity, and presenting a minimal set of safety-relevant information to the driver on that dashboard.

{\Large Abstract}\\
\hrule

% Place your abstract here.
This report describes an embedded telemetry unit for the TUKZIE Rev 0, a 72\,V electric cargo trike, and its integration with the vehicle's existing dashboard, which replaced the windshield head-up display of the original brief. An ESP32-S3 with an integrated LTE modem samples inertial measurement units at 200\,Hz (two on one board for the bench tests; from 22 October one on it and one on a second board at the front of the vehicle), reads the battery-management system over Bluetooth Low Energy and GNSS, computes time-domain ride features at the edge, logs every record to flash and publishes it over MQTT; after an outage it replays the records the broker did not acknowledge, and a program on the dashboard's Raspberry Pi~5 stores each record once in a database. Two camera units each run a pretrained object detector fused with three time-of-flight sensors and warn of objects closing on the vehicle from their time to contact: the Raspberry Pi~5 beside the display faces forward, and a Raspberry Pi~4 beside the battery, aligned in time with the inertial stream by a pulse wire, faces backward. Pages added to the dashboard switch between the two views and show live data only, with missing data marked as absent. On the bench, both inertial sensors held 200\,Hz with no lost samples, although each flash write, about once a minute, delays sampling by up to 44\,ms; calibration brought both to 9.80 to 9.81\,m/s\textsuperscript{2} at rest in the orientation calibrated; GNSS positions were a median of 2.7\,m from a phone reference; after a 10-minute radio outage the unit reconnected in 5.2\,s and cleared its 60-record backlog within 43\,s, and every one of the 111 records in its log reached the database, 24 of them by a backfill from the log; and with the whole system running, the detector on the Raspberry Pi~4 processed 14.6 frames/s with a median of 118 to 122\,ms from camera readout to detection (19.5 frames/s and 102\,ms for the detector alone), against 19.2 frames/s and 51 to 54\,ms on the Raspberry Pi~5 beside the running dashboard; mounted on the vehicle, the front detector returned results a median of 53 to 102\,ms after readout and the rear one 126 to 151\,ms. On a first road session with about 16 minutes of driving the battery link held under loads of up to 46.9\,A and no inertial samples were dropped, but 38\,\% of the per-second records did not reach the recorder over the USB link, which has since been replaced by a wired serial link. With no GNSS antenna fitted and no raw samples kept, the ride-characterisation and glance-time questions remain open.
