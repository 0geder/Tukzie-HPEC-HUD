\chapter{Discussion}
\label{chap:discussion}

This chapter interprets the results against the literature and the research questions. Its main limitation is stated throughout: the results are from the bench.

\section{Acquisition integrity and what it costs}

The literature review found that edge-telemetry studies are usually judged by accuracy or mean latency, without jitter, queue behaviour or outage recovery. The results here report those quantities directly: zero dropped samples, the worst gap with and without logging, and the cause of the only recurring gap. The flash-write pause shows why they matter. Buffering cut its rate sixfold but not its length, because the pause comes from the flash itself. A 40\,ms gap in a 200\,Hz stream is eight missing sample instants once a minute, small for 1\,s ride windows but significant for impact detection. Logging to separate storage, or on the Raspberry Pi, would remove it rather than reduce it. The open sensor settings belong to the same requirement: the 260\,Hz filter of the earlier bench sessions let content above 100\,Hz alias into the band (v0.7.4, used for T15 and the road test, has 94\,Hz), and the $\pm 8\,g$ range was probably reached by a hand disturbance, so clipped samples should be flagged before vehicle data are interpreted.

\section{Ride characterisation (RQ1, RQ4)}

The calibration result supports the review's warning that low-cost inertial measurements depend on the individual sensor and its mounting: two nominally identical units disagreed by about 8\% in one session and read 10.03 and 9.90\,m/s\textsuperscript{2} at calibration in the next, and their factors changed between boards because one magnitude factor holds only for one orientation. A ride index compared across sessions therefore has to be calibrated in its final mounting, which the firmware supports with one command. The features are computed correctly, but on the magnitude rather than a vehicle-fixed vertical axis and without ISO~2631 weighting, so they describe overall vibration exposure, not comfort. The equal-weight fusion and the disagreement figure are designed to show when the two mounting points see different motion; a $D$ of 1.68 in the bench session shows that this happens even on a hand-held board, and it will be the first thing to examine on the vehicle.

RQ4 cannot be answered from the bench. The method is in place: with the motor's Hall pulses giving its speed, vibration that follows motor speed with the vehicle on stands can be separated from vibration that appears only on the road, as the review recommends \cite{li2018}. It depends on the Hall tap and on the first vehicle sessions.

The first road session (19 October) supports two findings. On the vehicle no inertial samples were dropped and the rate held except for one stall of IMU1 of about 2\,s, although a third of the per-second records were lost on the USB link to the recorder; and driving and parked are separated clearly by the per-second vibration value together with the battery current. It cannot yet answer the questions that matter most. Without GNSS speed, raw samples or marked runs, neither the repeatability over repeated traversals (RQ1) nor the separation of powertrain from road vibration (RQ4) can be assessed. A second, shorter session is planned with the raw sample stream, the GNSS antenna connected (it was not fitted on the first), timed runs and the stationary powertrain baseline.

\section{Hazard sensing}

At the detector the camera pipeline is fast enough for its purpose: an alert about 0.26\,s after an object appears is about 2.2\,m of travel at 30\,km/h, although the delay to the screen is unmeasured. The weak point is distance, not speed. The first fused hazard put a person at 0.874\,m by the camera's width heuristic and 0.096\,m by the time-of-flight sensor, so a camera-only band can be a whole band wrong at close range; this is the case for fusion, in which the camera says what and where and the sensor says how far. The stand-in sensors' 1.2\,m range is only about 0.14\,s of travel at 30\,km/h, so they serve low-speed manoeuvring, and the camera remains the only source at road speed until longer-range sensing is fitted. The pothole models show the same dependence on local data that the review noted for roughness classifiers: a model scoring 0.597 on its own source found almost none of the potholes in South African footage, and training on the road band of that footage raised the validation score to 0.714. The held-out score and a run on the vehicle's own camera, which has no infrared-cut filter, are needed before that improvement can be claimed.

Two later changes address the weaknesses above. First, the time to contact from scale change (Section~\ref{sec:ttc}) turns the camera's least reliable output, distance, into one that needs no calibration. That fits the review's finding that monocular distance is the weak link and that production warnings rely on scale change instead \cite{dagan2004fcw}. The time-of-flight sensors supply the same measure inside their short range, so at road speed the warning still rests on the camera. Its accuracy is untested (C11), and a stationary object ahead counts as closing while the vehicle moves, which is the intended forward-collision behaviour, not a fault. Second, moving detection to the Raspberry Pi~5 beside the display (Section~\ref{sec:two-sided}) cut the median from readout to result from 118 to 122\,ms (Raspberry Pi~4 with the whole system) to 51 to 54\,ms (Raspberry Pi~5 beside the dashboard). The two bench runs did not carry identical loads, but on the vehicle the front unit ran at 53 to 102\,ms against 126 to 151\,ms at the rear, so the gain held. A front alert no longer crosses the network to reach the screen. At 30\,km/h that is about 0.6\,m less travel before the alert, the same order as the stand-in sensors' whole range. The price is a second processor's load beside the dashboard (64 to 73\,\% of the Raspberry Pi~5's CPU in total) and a rear unit whose own detector, on the Raspberry Pi~4, remains the slower path.

\section{Data integrity and connectivity (RQ3)}

Logging independently of the connection, and reconnecting when the modem reports that data are available, gave the store-and-forward behaviour the review identified as necessary for intermittent coverage. The read-back is the important result: it recovered every record and found a fault, invalid JSON for missing values, that watching the live stream had not shown. With v0.7.4 the path reaches a database: after a 10-minute outage every logged record was stored once and the backlog cleared within a minute, which gives the completeness, duplicate and recovery measures of Chapter~\ref{chap:lit}. On the public broker, however, an acknowledgement does not mean that a subscriber received the record, so completeness rests on comparing the database with the unit's log, as it did for 24 records; a broker that queues for a persistent subscriber would remove that dependence. Delay needs GNSS time and is left to the road test.

\section{Driver display (RQ2)}

The choice of the existing dashboard over a windshield HUD is supported by the review: a head-up position does not make a display safe by itself, its optics are hard to make legible on a removable unit in daylight, and a second display adds a competing channel. Of the five dashboard issues, data validity and update age were the ones that integration showed to matter most: the inherited dashboard showed simulated speed and battery values without any sign whenever its data link was down, which is worse than showing nothing, and live-only mode with a 3\,s age limit removes that. Layout and alert priority follow the review's guidance on few elements and a stable hierarchy, with only immediate hazards interrupting the driving page. Contrast is the weakest of the five: the interface was restyled with dark ink on a light ground, a single tap colour and status words beside every colour mark (Appendix~\ref{app:interface}), but it has not been measured in daylight. With two cameras the same rules decide what interrupts the driver. The banner names the side of each warning and puts an approaching object before a merely close one, and the Surroundings page shows one side at a time, so a second camera does not become a second competing display. The touch measurement shows that the panel is not a source of delay, so the slowness noticed comes from the buttons acting on release, which can be changed. Whether the display can be read within the 2\,s guidance has not been measured; the character-size and touch-target figures of Section~\ref{sec:lit-display} give a check that can be applied before the on-vehicle observation.

\section{Integration and power}

The most consequential finding of the integration was not in any one subsystem. The telemetry unit's LTE modem, powered from the Raspberry Pi's USB port, pulled the supply into under-voltage whenever it transmitted, throttled the processor and slowed the detector, and on its USB port the Raspberry Pi~4 restarted three times in 20 minutes, against none in 4 hours after the unit was moved (Section~\ref{sec:inv-power}). The modem worked correctly throughout; the fault was in how the parts were powered together. A separate supply for the telemetry unit is therefore a requirement for the vehicle installation. The same fault returned once the Raspberry Pi~4 was closed in its printed box with the unit still on its USB port: it brought heat to 76.9\,\textdegree C, throttling, a brown-out restart and a rise in lost dashboard lines. So the enclosure, the supply and the cooling have to be designed together, which led to the vented lids.

Placement shaped the architecture as much as processing did. The battery-management link only held once the telemetry unit sat beside the pack, and that, with the Raspberry Pi~5's faster detection, produced the two-sided layout: front sensing beside the display, rear sensing and the telemetry unit beside the battery, joined by a cable. The power budget (Table~\ref{tab:power-budget}) puts the added electronics at about 20\,W, under 1\,\% of the motor's rated input, so the draw while driving is small. The real requirements are switching off with the vehicle and a converter for the pack's 60 to 88\,V. Only the Raspberry Pi~5's share is measured; the rest are estimates until each unit's current is read.

\section{Validity and limitations}

Almost all results come from one set of hardware on the bench, with one road session, without the vehicle's vibration, heat, supply and lighting. Several parameters are still defaults or uncalibrated: the camera's focal length, the time-of-flight directions, the accelerometer range and filter, and the GNSS speed units. The pothole score is on validation tiles. These limits do not invalidate the bench results, which measure what they claim to measure, but they bound what can be concluded from them.
