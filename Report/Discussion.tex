\chapter{Discussion}
\label{chap:discussion}

This chapter interprets the results of Chapter~\ref{chap:results} against the literature of Chapter~\ref{chap:lit} and the research questions of Chapter~\ref{chap:intro}. Most of the evidence comes from the bench and the parked or raised vehicle, with one road session so far, so each section states how far its conclusion should hold on the road; Section~\ref{sec:disc-validity} draws the common limits together.

\section{Acquisition integrity and what it costs (RQ1, RQ3)}
\label{sec:disc-acq}

Edge-telemetry studies are rarely judged by jitter, queue behaviour or outage recovery; the results here report them directly. Buffering cut the flash-write pause's rate sixfold but not its length, because the pause comes from the flash itself. A 40\,ms gap is eight missing sample instants once a minute, small for 1\,s ride windows but significant for impact detection; logging elsewhere would remove it. The stall of 21 and 22 October, in which drop counts rose while the rates still read 200\,Hz, makes the same point: the counters exposed a fault the headline rate hid. It was traced to the statistics and ride tasks being starved on one core, possibly by the second IMU's long wiring, and v0.7.6, which moved them, later rode through a real bus failure without a stall.

\section{Ride characterisation (RQ1, RQ4)}
\label{sec:disc-ride}

The calibration result supports the review's warning that low-cost inertial measurements depend on the individual sensor and its mounting: two nominally identical units disagreed by about 8\% in one session and read 10.03 and 9.90\,m/s\textsuperscript{2} at calibration in the next (Section~\ref{sec:resx-cal}), and one magnitude factor holds for one orientation only. A ride measure compared across sessions therefore has to be calibrated in its final mounting. The on-board features are correct but computed on the magnitude, so they describe overall vibration exposure. The analysis for the second session adds what the review asks for: a vertical axis from the gravity direction, the ISO~2631-1 weighting kept separate from the response features, and repeatability expressed as a CV and an ICC with intervals.

RQ4 needs the vehicle. The method follows the review \cite{li2018}: stand runs identify the powertrain's lines, and coasting passes at matched speed show what remains on the road without traction. The stand runs of 23 October put the powertrain's energy between about 40 and 70\,Hz at the rear and found its lines at the front as well (Figure~\ref{fig:res-stands}), so a front sensor does not escape it. The front-to-rear delay gives a speed that needs no GNSS, but only on full-width features, because the front and rear wheels run on different tracks.

The first road session showed acquisition holding on the vehicle and the vibration value with the battery current separating driving from parked clearly, but without a speed reference, raw samples or marked runs it could not answer RQ1 or RQ4; the second session was designed around those three gaps.

The 27 October passes answer RQ1 on the road in part. On one smooth loop at one speed the instrument repeats within about 5\,\% (CV of $a_w$, n = 5, at both nodes), inside the 10\,\% criterion, and it separated two road surfaces at the same speed by a factor of about two, so it responds to the road rather than drifting between passes. Three limits remain: the pass windows come from GNSS speed, not section lines or video, so they include turns and run-ups; too few passes were driven at 15 and 20\,km/h for their repeatability or an ICC; and $I_v$ is not speed-invariant, so indices from different speeds cannot be compared directly. For RQ4, the powertrain's lines carry only 1 to 3\,\% of the road energy and coasting left $a_w$ unchanged at 10\,km/h, so at these speeds the vibration is dominated by the road and the body's 4 to 8\,Hz response, not the motor; at higher power the 40 to 70\,Hz energy of the stand runs would weigh more. No pothole or bump passes were marked, so the pothole detector is not scored.

\section{Hazard sensing (RQ2, RQ3)}
\label{sec:disc-hazard}

At the detector the camera pipeline is fast enough: an alert about 0.26\,s after an object appears is about 2.2\,m of travel at 30\,km/h, although the delay to the screen is unmeasured. The weak point is distance. The first fused hazard put the camera's width estimate 0.78\,m from the time-of-flight reading, a whole band wrong at close range, which is the case for fusion. The stand-in sensors' configured 1.2\,m range is about 0.14\,s of travel at 30\,km/h, so at road speed the camera is the only source. The parked checks of 26 October put numbers on this: the camera's box width scaled correctly with distance, but its range of a person depends on an assumed width, which read 38\,\% short until it was changed, while the time-of-flight reading at 0.5\,m set the fused distance within 3\,cm. The pothole models show the dependence on local data that the review noted: a model with a mAP@50 of 0.597 on its own source found almost none of the potholes in South African footage (Table~\ref{tab:res-camera}), and training on that footage raised the validation score to 0.714, not yet claimed because no held-out or on-vehicle score exists.

Two later changes address these weaknesses. Time to contact from scale change (Section~\ref{sec:ttc}) replaces distance, the camera's least reliable output, with a measure that needs no calibration, as production warnings do \cite{dagan2004fcw}; its accuracy is untested (C11). Moving detection to the Raspberry Pi~5 (Section~\ref{sec:two-sided}) cut the median from readout to result from 118 to 122\,ms to 51 to 54\,ms on the bench, and on the vehicle the front ran at 53 to 102\,ms against 126 to 151\,ms at the rear, 0.4 to 0.6\,m less travel at 30\,km/h, the same order as the stand-in sensors' range. The price is load beside the dashboard (Table~\ref{tab:res-camera}). How many road alerts were correct is unknown: the first session kept no video to check them.

No forward video of the session was kept, so the alerts on the vehicle remain unchecked against ground truth; precision and false alerts per kilometre are future work (Section~\ref{sec:recs-next}).

\section{Data integrity and connectivity (RQ3)}
\label{sec:disc-data}

Logging independently of the connection gave the store-and-forward behaviour the review called for. The read-back recovered all 6,020 records on the bench (2,000 of 2,022 from the vehicle's unit) and found a fault, invalid JSON for missing values, that the live stream had not shown. After a 10-minute outage every logged record reached the database once and the backlog cleared within a minute, which gives the completeness, duplicate and recovery measures of Chapter~\ref{chap:lit}. On the public broker an acknowledgement does not mean a subscriber received the record, so completeness rests on comparing the database with the unit's log. Latency and completeness on the moving vehicle were not measured: the first session had no GNSS time and no reachable broker. They are future work (Chapter~\ref{chap:recs}).

\section{Driver display (RQ2)}
\label{sec:disc-display}

The review supports the existing dashboard over a windshield HUD, which would add a competing channel. Of the five dashboard issues, data validity mattered most: the inherited dashboard showed simulated values without any sign when its link was down, which live-only mode with a 3\,s age limit removes. Layout and alert priority follow the guidance on few elements and a stable hierarchy, with only immediate hazards interrupting the driving page; the banner names the side of each warning and puts approaching objects first, and the Surroundings page shows one side at a time, so the second camera does not become a competing display. Contrast is the weakest issue: the design uses dark ink on a light ground with words beside every colour mark (Appendix~\ref{app:interface}), but it has not been measured against ISO~15008's minimum contrast ratios of 3:1 by day and 2:1 in sunlight \cite{iso15008}, and paint times only on the laptop. Glance time needs a participant and was not measured, but the legibility check that needs none found the hierarchy inverted: the speed, which the driver can read from the instrument cluster anyway, is four times larger than ISO~15008 asks, while the warnings, the reason for the system, are at half its acceptable size. Enlarging the banner to about 24\,px capitals is the first change before any glance study.

\section{Integration and power}
\label{sec:disc-power}

The most consequential finding was in no single subsystem. The LTE modem, powered from the Raspberry Pi's USB port, pulled the supply into under-voltage whenever it transmitted, throttling the processor and slowing the detector; the Raspberry Pi~4 restarted three times in 20 minutes, against none in 4 hours once the unit was moved (Section~\ref{sec:inv-power}). It returned in the closed rear box, with heat and a brown-out restart (Section~\ref{sec:inv-power}), so enclosure, supply and cooling must be designed together; on its own LiPo the unit browned out during modem bursts, so it still needs a regulated supply. The added electronics draw about 20\,W (Table~\ref{tab:power-budget}), under 1\,\% of the motor's rated input; the real requirements are switching off with the vehicle and a converter for the pack's full voltage range, which depends on its unconfirmed chemistry. Only the Raspberry Pi~5's share is measured.

\section{Validity and limitations}
\label{sec:disc-validity}

Almost all results come from one set of hardware on the bench, and most are single runs (one outage test, one 62-minute soak, 60\,s detector runs, one road session), so they show what the system does, not its spread. Calibrated only in part: the person width (fitted on one person in one stance) and the time-of-flight sensor ahead (one approximate distance). Uncalibrated: the camera's focal length (600\,px set, about 635\,px expected), the side sensors' bearings and the GNSS speed units; the ISO~2631-1 weighting was checked against published derivations, not the standard itself.
