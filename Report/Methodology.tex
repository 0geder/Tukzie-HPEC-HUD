Each IMU task runs at the highest priority on core~1 and holds a 5\,ms period with \texttt{vTaskDelayUntil}, which schedules each wake-up from the previous target, so execution time does not accumulate into drift. \subsection{Sampling and anti-aliasing}
\label{sec:sampling}

The MPU6050 low-pass filters its accelerometer and updates its output registers at 1\,kHz \cite{invensense2012rm}; reading them every 5\,ms decimates that stream to $f_s = 200$\,Hz with no further filtering. A component at frequency $f$ appears at
\begin{equation}
f_a = \lvert f - k f_s \rvert, \qquad k = \operatorname{round}(f/f_s),
\label{eq:alias}
\end{equation}
so anything between 100 and 200\,Hz folds to $200 - f$. The setting used, 94\,Hz bandwidth with 3.0\,ms delay \cite{invensense2012rm}, is the widest below the 100\,Hz Nyquist frequency; the next, 44\,Hz, would attenuate the top of the 80\,Hz ISO band and the powertrain lines that RQ4 needs. The register map does not give the filter's order. The lines measured on the stands at 42.75, 49.0 and 64.0\,Hz (Figure~\ref{fig:res-stands}) could be images of 157.25, 151.0 and 136.0\,Hz, which a first-order response at 94\,Hz attenuates by only 5.8, 5.5 and 4.9\,dB, and a second-order Butterworth response by 9.5, 8.8 and 7.3\,dB. A line 10\,dB above the floor, the powertrain criterion, could therefore be the image of a stronger component. Speed separates them: a motor order $f = k\Omega$ rises with wheel speed $\Omega$ while its image $f_s - k\Omega$ falls, which stand runs at three settings with a wheel video resolve (Section~\ref{sec:impl-run2}). The 3.0\,ms filter delay is the same on both nodes and cancels in the front-to-rear delay.

\subsection{Riding through the flash-write pause}
\label{sec:buffering}

A flash write suspends code running from flash on both cores for $T_p = 33$ to 44\,ms (Table~\ref{tab:res-acquisition}), stopping producers and consumers alike. Afterwards each IMU task runs its missed slots back to back, so every queue must accept
\begin{equation}
n_p = \lceil T_p f_s \rceil = \lceil 0.044 \times 200 \rceil = 9
\label{eq:buffer}
\end{equation}
samples on top of its normal fill. The IMU queues hold 100 samples, eleven times $n_p$, so a consumer may stall for 0.5\,s before a sample is lost; the ride-feature and raw-stream queues hold 400 and 200. The catch-up limit of 20 periods is 2.3 times the longest pause, so the pause is caught up while a longer stall, such as a failing bus, gives up its slots. Writing the log once a minute makes the pause rarer, not shorter. The sensor's FIFO is not used, so the caught-up reads are new readings, not the missed instants: each pause leaves a hole of up to 44\,ms, 4.4\,\% of a window, visible in the timestamps. Reading the FIFO would fill it.

\section{Ride-characterisation signal processing}
\label{sec:ride-characterisation}

This section serves R2 and R3, and RQ1 and RQ4. A task per IMU computes time-domain features from each one-second window of $N = 200$ samples ($\Delta t = 1/f_s$). The mounting has not been related to a vehicle frame, so the orientation-free magnitude is used:
\begin{equation}
a_k = s\,\sqrt{a_{x,k}^2 + a_{y,k}^2 + a_{z,k}^2} \approx g + a_{v,k} + \frac{\lvert \mathbf{a}_{h,k} \rvert^2}{2g},
\label{eq:magnitude}
\end{equation}
where $s$ is the sensor's calibration factor and $a_{v,k}$ and $\mathbf{a}_{h,k}$ are the dynamic acceleration along and across gravity. While the dynamic acceleration is small against $g$, the magnitude's fluctuation is the vertical vibration, plus a second-order term from horizontal motion. From the window,
\begin{align}
\mu &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N} a_k, &
a_{\mathrm{RMS}} &= \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N} a_k^2}, &
\sigma &= \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N} a_k^2 - \mu^2}, \label{eq:mean-rms-std}\\
a_{\mathrm{pp}} &= \max_k a_k - \min_k a_k, &
\mathrm{CF} &= \frac{\max_k a_k}{a_{\mathrm{RMS}}}, &
\bar{J} &= \frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{k=2}^{N} \frac{\lvert a_k - a_{k-1}\rvert}{\Delta t}, \label{eq:pp-cf-jerk}
\end{align}
and the threshold count is the number of samples with $a_k > \mu + 2\sigma$. Since $a_k$ includes gravity, $\sigma$ is the vibration measure used. The ISO~2631-1 weighting is not applied on the unit, so these features describe vibration exposure, not comfort; the offline analysis computes the weighted value (Section~\ref{sec:road-analysis}). A synthetic sine checks the code at every boot (Section~\ref{sec:impl-ride}).

\subsection{Per-sensor calibration}
\label{sec:imu-magnitude-calibration}

Two nominally identical sensors at rest read about 8\,\% apart. One factor per sensor (Equation~\ref{eq:calibration}), from 200 stationary readings and kept in non-volatile storage, corrects the magnitude; it is reused at boot, since the vehicle may be powered while moving, and recalibrated in place by command. It holds only in the orientation in which it was measured (Section~\ref{sec:impl-cal}).

\subsection{Speed-normalised index}
\label{sec:speed-norm}

The same road driven faster gives a larger response. Following Li et al., who classify roughness from acceleration mean square divided by speed \cite{li2018}, each window also computes
\begin{equation}
I_v = \frac{\sigma^2}{v}, \qquad v \ge v_{\min},
\label{eq:speed-index}
\end{equation}
where $v$ is the GNSS speed. The $1/v$ scaling follows from a stated model. ISO~8608 describes a road profile by its displacement spectral density in spatial frequency $n$ \cite{agostinacchio2014},
\begin{equation}
G_d(n) = G_d(n_0)\left(\frac{n}{n_0}\right)^{-w}, \qquad n_0 = 0.1\,\mathrm{cycles/m}, \quad w = 2.
\label{eq:road-psd}
\end{equation}
At speed $v$ the wheel meets temporal frequency $f = vn$, and conserving variance gives $G_q(f) = G_d(f/v)/v$, so the vertical input velocity has the white spectral density
\begin{equation}
G_{\dot q}(f) = (2\pi f)^2 G_q(f) = 4\pi^2 n_0^2\, G_d(n_0)\, v .
\label{eq:road-velocity}
\end{equation}
For a response linear in the input, with $H(f)$ from input velocity to measured acceleration,
\begin{equation}
\sigma^2 = \int_0^{\infty} \lvert H(f) \rvert^2 G_{\dot q}(f)\,df = 4\pi^2 n_0^2\, G_d(n_0)\, v \int_0^{\infty} \lvert H(f) \rvert^2 df,
\label{eq:iv-derivation}
\end{equation}
so $\sigma^2/v$ depends on the road class and the vehicle, not on speed. The assumptions are the model's, not measured facts: $w = 2$ (otherwise $\sigma^2 \propto v^{w-1}$), a linear, stationary response, and $\sigma$ set by the road rather than the powertrain lines of Section~\ref{sec:sampling}. The second road session tests the consequence, $I_v$ constant from 10 to 20\,km/h (Section~\ref{sec:road-analysis}). The GNSS speed is unchecked and the sensors are on the body, not the unsprung mass, so $I_v$ supports relative comparison within one dataset, not a roughness class (Section~\ref{sec:impl-speed}).

\subsection{Two-sensor comparison and speed from the front-to-rear delay}
\label{sec:imu-fusion}

A road feature reaches the two ends at different times, so the nodes are compared, not averaged (the earlier averaging is in Section~\ref{sec:impl-fusion}). The analysis gives per-sensor features over common windows and the front-to-rear delay $\tau_d$, the lag of the cross-correlation peak of front and rear vertical acceleration, refined by a parabola. With wheelbase $L$,
\begin{equation}
v = \frac{L}{\tau_d}, \qquad
\left(\frac{\delta v}{v}\right)^2 \approx \left(\frac{\delta L}{L}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\delta \tau_d}{\tau_d}\right)^2, \qquad
\frac{\delta \tau_d}{\tau_d} = \frac{v\,\delta t}{L},
\label{eq:delay-speed}
\end{equation}
with no GNSS. The timing error $\delta t$ (Table~\ref{tab:delay-budget}) sums to at most 5.3\,ms, 1.8\,\% of a 0.3\,s delay, growing in proportion to speed; a wheelbase taped to $\pm 1$\,cm adds 1\,\% for a 1\,m wheelbase, less for a longer one. Both are well inside the proposed 10\,\% criterion against the video speed, so timing does not limit the method; correlation does, because the front wheel and the two rear wheels run on different tracks and only full-width features such as a speed bump are used (Section~\ref{sec:impl-road}).

\section{Camera subsystem}
\label{sec:camera-subsystem}

This section serves R6, R7, R8, R10 and R11, and RQ1, RQ2 and RQ3.

\subsection{Capture and synchronisation}
\label{sec:sync-pulse}

The camera gives a visual record against which inertial events can be labelled, but it is on another board than the IMU. Each ESP32 therefore toggles a pin every 500\,ms and logs the microsecond time of each edge, and its Raspberry Pi timestamps each edge through the kernel's GPIO character device, on the monotonic clock of the camera's frame timestamps \cite{picamera2manual}. A straight line fitted to the edge pairs maps node time to Pi time, with offset and drift: the measured 500.06\,ms median interval means about 120 parts per million, 0.4\,s an hour (Section~\ref{sec:impl-sync}).

\subsection{Detection}
\label{sec:hazard-detection}

The detector runs a pretrained SSD-MobileNet-v1 \cite{liu2016ssd, howard2017mobilenets}, trained on COCO \cite{lin2014coco} and quantised to 8-bit integers \cite{jacob2018quantization} for LiteRT \cite{litert}, which needs no training data and no accelerator (Section~\ref{sec:lit-detection}). It reports vehicles, cyclists, pedestrians and animals in three distance bands from the box width and an assumed object width; the focal length is not calibrated, so the bands are indicative. An alert needs three consecutive frames and clears after five without the object. Each alert keeps five fields (class, band, confidence, time, status); frames stay in memory, nothing recognises faces or plates, inference is local and the output is advisory. A YOLO11n pothole model \cite{ultralytics2024yolo11, gerapothole, nienaber2015dataset} is an experimental path, off by default, because it slows the detector \cite{ncnn} (Section~\ref{sec:impl-detect}).

\subsection{Range sensing and fusion}
\label{sec:tof-sensor}
\label{sec:camera-tof-fusion}

The chosen VL53L5CX (8$\times$8 zones) had not arrived, so three VL53L0X sensors facing left, ahead and right stand in on each camera unit, on the Pi's own bus; one that does not answer is skipped and looked for again (Section~\ref{sec:inv-tof}). The camera gives class and bearing but not distance; a time-of-flight sensor gives distance close by, without a class. A detection whose bearing falls in a sensor's cone, with a fresh valid reading, takes the camera's class and the sensor's distance; an unmatched detection keeps the camera estimate, and an unmatched reading becomes an unclassified obstacle. Association is frame by frame, without the detector's tracks (Section~\ref{sec:ttc}), and the sensor directions are defaults, not measurements (Section~\ref{sec:impl-tof}, Figure~\ref{fig:fusion-geometry}).

\subsection{Approaching objects: time to contact}
\label{sec:ttc}

A distance band says how near an object is, not whether it is closing. A tracker in the detector follows each detection across frames by box overlap, and the time to contact comes from the growth of its image width $w$, the expansion measure of Section~\ref{sec:lit-detection} \cite{lee1976tau,dagan2004fcw}. For an object of width $W$ at range $Z$ closing at constant speed, $w = fW/Z$, so $u = 1/w$ falls linearly to zero at contact and
\begin{equation}
\tau = \frac{w}{\dot w} = -\frac{u}{\dot u},
\label{eq:ttc}
\end{equation}
which needs neither the focal length $f$ nor $W$, both uncalibrated. A Theil-Sen line \cite{sen1968}, the median of pairwise slopes, which tolerates an outlying box, is fitted to $u$ against the frames' sensor timestamps over the last $T = 0.6$\,s, and $\tau$ is taken at the newest frame. With relative box-width noise $\varepsilon$ and $n = f_c T$ frames at frame rate $f_c$, a least-squares line, used as an approximation for the Theil-Sen fit, gives the slope a relative error $\varepsilon (\tau/T)\sqrt{12/n}$ and the end value $2\varepsilon/\sqrt{n}$; the slope dominates by $\sqrt{3}\,\tau/T$ (11.5 at 4\,s), so
\begin{equation}
\frac{\sigma_\tau}{\tau} \approx \frac{\sigma_{\dot w}}{\dot w} \approx \varepsilon\,\frac{\tau}{T}\sqrt{\frac{12}{n}}.
\label{eq:ttc-error}
\end{equation}
At the 4\,s threshold this is $6.8\varepsilon$ on the Raspberry Pi~5 (19.2\,frames/s, $n \approx 11.5$) and $7.8\varepsilon$ on the Raspberry Pi~4 (14.6\,frames/s, $n \approx 8.8$), half that at 2\,s: a 1\,\% box jitter gives about $\pm 0.3$\,s at 4\,s. The error falls as $T^{-3/2}$, so a longer window is more precise but slower to follow a change in closing speed. The width grows by the fraction $T/\tau$ over the window, so the 5\,\% growth gate admits $\tau$ up to 12\,s, three times the warning threshold. Test C11, not yet run, will measure $\varepsilon$. At close range the time-of-flight sensors give their own closing time. A stationary object ahead of the moving vehicle approaches, which is intended: a forward-collision warning (Section~\ref{sec:impl-ttc}).

\subsection{Two-sided layout}
\label{sec:two-sided}

Sensing is split by side. The Raspberry Pi~5 behind the dashboard runs the front camera, detector and time-of-flight board, so a front alert does not cross the network and has the Pi~5's lower latency (median 51 to 54 against 102\,ms, Section~\ref{sec:res-hazard}). The Raspberry Pi~4 beside the battery looks backward and reads the rear node near the pack. A dedicated Ethernet cable joins them, leaving Wi-Fi for the internet. The dashboard switches between Front and Rear, the rear view mirrored, and its banner shows both sides (Figure~\ref{fig:two-sided}; Section~\ref{sec:impl-twosided}).

\section{Maintenance design}
\label{sec:maintenance}

This section serves R13 and supports RQ3. Firmware is built from the repository with the board settings fixed in \texttt{platformio.ini}, and every record carries its firmware version; the Raspberry Pis are set up by deploy scripts that record the deployed commit; every program runs as a service that restarts on failure; tests travel with the code; the display and pods come off without tools; and every wire is listed (Appendix~\ref{app:wiring}). There is no over-the-air update, and removal from the vehicle has not been timed (Section~\ref{sec:impl-maint}).

The first road session ran on an earlier design (v0.7.4, both IMUs on the rear unit; Section~\ref{sec:impl-fusion}); the bench tests from 22 October ran on this one. Chapter~\ref{chap:results} gives the results.
