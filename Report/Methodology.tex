\chapter{Methodology}
\label{chap:method}

This chapter describes the system as built and how each part was verified. Development followed a staged approach: each interface was tested on its own before higher-level functions were added, so that a fault could be traced to one change. The faults that shaped the design are given here as short findings; the full development record, with every fault, its cause and its fix, is in Appendix~\ref{app:devrecord}, and the firmware configuration (versions, pins, bus speeds, task priorities, queue depths and partitions) is in Table~\ref{tab:config} of that appendix.

\section{System overview and architecture selection}
\label{sec:architecture}

Figure~\ref{fig:system-architecture} shows the system. An ESP32-S3 telemetry unit samples two inertial measurement units (IMUs), reads GNSS and the battery-management system (BMS), computes ride features, logs locally and publishes over LTE. A Raspberry Pi~4 at the front of the vehicle runs the camera detector, reads three time-of-flight sensors, fuses the two, and bridges the telemetry unit's data to the network. The vehicle's existing dashboard on a Raspberry Pi~5 reads both over Wi-Fi.

\section{Hardware}
\label{sec:hardware}
\label{sec:camera-mount}

The telemetry unit is a Makerfabs ESP32-S3 4G LTE CAT1 A7670X board: an ESP32-S3-WROOM-1-N16R8 module (16\,MB flash, 8\,MB PSRAM) with an A7670X LTE Cat-1 modem that also contains the GNSS receiver \cite{makerfabsa7670x} (Figure~\ref{fig:makerfabs-pinout}, Appendix~\ref{app:figures}). The integrated board lets processor, modem and GNSS be tested without a separate modem circuit. Two MPU6050 IMUs are on separate I\textsuperscript{2}C buses at 400\,kHz, which avoids their shared address and bus contention. The modem is on a hardware UART at 115200\,bit/s, with its power key and reset on GPIO~4 and~5. The board's microSD slot was not used: its chip-select line passes through a resistor (R23) that the schematic marks as not fitted, so local logging uses the internal flash (Section~\ref{sec:local-logging}).

The Raspberry Pi~4 carries an OV5647 camera module without an infrared-cut filter on a 15\,cm ribbon, three VL53L0X time-of-flight sensors on its I\textsuperscript{2}C bus, and the sync-pulse input on GPIO17. The camera is housed in a three-part casing designed and 3D printed in this project (Figure~\ref{fig:camera-casing}, Appendix~\ref{app:figures}), not yet fixed to the vehicle. Every connection is listed in Appendix~\ref{app:wiring}, and Figure~\ref{fig:bench-setup} shows the bench setup.

\section{Firmware architecture}
\label{sec:dual-imu-architecture}
\label{sec:acquisition-instrumentation}

The firmware is written with PlatformIO and the Arduino framework on FreeRTOS (Table~\ref{tab:config}). Figure~\ref{fig:task-architecture} shows its tasks and queues. The two IMU tasks run at the highest priority on core~1, each holding a 5\,ms period with \texttt{vTaskDelayUntil}, which schedules each wake-up from the previous target time so that variable execution time does not accumulate into drift. The modem, BMS and dashboard-line tasks run on core~0, away from acquisition.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\input{figures/tikz/firmware_tasks}
\caption{FreeRTOS tasks and queues (drawn for firmware v0.7.1; unchanged in v0.7.3). Priorities as set in the firmware (higher runs first); each queue is labelled with its name and capacity in items. Queues between the two cores are drawn in blue.}
\label{fig:task-architecture}
\end{figure}

The 200\,Hz rate follows from ISO~2631-1, which weights vibration up to 80\,Hz \cite{iso2631}: sampling must exceed 160\,Hz, and 200\,Hz adds margin without loading the bus heavily. Each sample carries a sensor identifier, a per-sensor sequence number and a timestamp from \texttt{esp\_timer\_get\_time()}, a monotonic microsecond clock unaffected by network time. No acquisition task prints, because serial output blocks for a variable time and would disturb the period being measured. When a queue is full the producer drops the new sample and counts it rather than blocking, so a slow consumer can never hold up sampling. Each IMU feeds two queues, one to the statistics task and one to its ride-characterisation task, so that adding processing cannot change the acquisition accounting.

A lower-priority statistics task drains the queues and reports, every 2\,s and per sensor, the achieved rate, the minimum, mean and maximum interval between samples and the drop count, with the GNSS poll and fix counts. These counters produce the acquisition results of Chapter~\ref{chap:results} without a separate measurement pass, on the bench or on the vehicle.

Each IMU is set to $\pm 8\,g$, $\pm 500^{\circ}$/s and its widest digital low-pass filter, 260\,Hz, so that the 80\,Hz band of interest is not attenuated. The $\pm 8\,g$ range gives headroom for pothole and kerb impacts while keeping resolution; it is provisional, to be set from the peaks the vehicle produces. Two limits follow: samples at the $\pm 8\,g$ limit are not flagged, so a clipped window under-reports its peaks; and 260\,Hz is above the 100\,Hz Nyquist frequency, so vibration between 100 and 260\,Hz can alias into the band. The next filter setting, 94\,Hz, limits aliasing at a small cost in the 80\,Hz band.

\section{Ride-characterisation signal processing}
\label{sec:ride-characterisation}

A task per IMU computes time-domain features from each one-second window. Features use the acceleration magnitude rather than one axis because the mounting has not been related to a vehicle frame; the magnitude does not depend on orientation, at the cost of not saying which axis a disturbance came from. Each window holds $N = 200$ samples ($f_s = 200$\,Hz, $\Delta t = 1/f_s$). The calibrated magnitude of sample $k$ is
\begin{equation}
a_k = s\,\sqrt{a_{x,k}^2 + a_{y,k}^2 + a_{z,k}^2},
\label{eq:magnitude}
\end{equation}
where $s$ is the sensor's calibration factor. From the window,
\begin{align}
\mu &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N} a_k, &
a_{\mathrm{RMS}} &= \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N} a_k^2}, &
\sigma &= \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N} a_k^2 - \mu^2}, \label{eq:mean-rms-std}\\
a_{\mathrm{pp}} &= \max_k a_k - \min_k a_k, &
\mathrm{CF} &= \frac{\max_k a_k}{a_{\mathrm{RMS}}}, &
\bar{J} &= \frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{k=2}^{N} \frac{\lvert a_k - a_{k-1}\rvert}{\Delta t}, \label{eq:pp-cf-jerk}
\end{align}
and the threshold count is the number of samples with $a_k > \mu + 2\sigma$. Because $a_k$ includes gravity, $a_{\mathrm{RMS}}$ is close to 9.8\,m/s\textsuperscript{2} at rest and the crest factor close to 1; $\sigma$ removes the constant part and is the vibration measure used. Lost samples are counted, not interpolated. The ISO~2631-1 frequency weighting is not implemented, so the features describe vibration exposure, not comfort as the standard defines it. At every boot the feature code is checked against a synthetic 5\,Hz sine of 200 samples, whose RMS, peak-to-peak and crest factor have closed-form values ($A/\sqrt{2}$, $2A$, $\sqrt{2}$), with a tolerance of 5\%.

\subsection{Per-sensor calibration}
\label{sec:imu-magnitude-calibration}

Two nominally identical sensors at rest on the same platform read 10.35 to 10.39 and 9.55 to 9.56\,m/s\textsuperscript{2}, about 5.7\% above and 2.6\% below standard gravity, a disagreement of about 8\%. Since only the magnitude is used, one scale factor per sensor corrects it: RMS, $\sigma$, peak-to-peak and jerk scale linearly with it, and crest factor and the threshold count are unaffected. The first time a sensor is found with no stored factor, the firmware averages 200 stationary readings and stores
\begin{equation}
s = \frac{g_0}{\frac{1}{200}\sum_{k=1}^{200} \sqrt{a_{x,k}^2 + a_{y,k}^2 + a_{z,k}^2}}, \qquad g_0 = 9.80665\,\mathrm{m/s^2},
\label{eq:calibration}
\end{equation}
in non-volatile storage, rejecting values outside 0.5 to 2.0 as evidence that the sensor was moving. The factor is reused at every boot rather than recomputed, since the vehicle may be powered while moving, and a console command erases it so the sensors can be recalibrated in their mounted position (not yet used). A single factor corrects the reading only in the orientation in which it was measured, because per-axis offsets remain; this is why the factors changed between boards (Chapter~\ref{chap:results}).

\subsection{Speed-normalised index}
\label{sec:speed-norm}

The same road driven faster gives a larger response. Following Li et al., who classify roughness from acceleration mean square divided by speed \cite{li2018}, each window also computes
\begin{equation}
R = \frac{\sigma^2}{v}, \qquad v \ge v_{\min},
\label{eq:speed-index}
\end{equation}
where $v$ is the latest GNSS speed and $v_{\min} = 1$ in the receiver's units; without a fix or below $v_{\min}$, $R$ is not computed. Firmware before v0.7.2 used $a_{\mathrm{RMS}}^2$, which is dominated by gravity; v0.7.2 uses the variance. Two limits apply: the receiver's speed units are not yet confirmed (Section~\ref{sec:gnss-parsing-validation}), so $R$ supports relative comparison within one dataset only; and the sensors are on the body, not the unsprung mass, so $R$ is a speed-normalised response measure, not a validated roughness class.

\subsection{Dual-IMU fusion}
\label{sec:imu-fusion}

The two sensors are the same part, configuration and calibration, so they are combined with equal weights: for RMS, $\sigma$, peak-to-peak, jerk and $R$, $x_f = (x_1 + x_2)/2$. Crest factor and the threshold count stay per sensor. Because the sensors are at different points on the vehicle, averaging would hide real differences, so each fused window also reports
\begin{equation}
D = \frac{\lvert \sigma_1 - \sigma_2 \rvert}{\max(\sigma_f,\ \sigma_{\mathrm{floor}})}, \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{floor}} = 0.05\,\mathrm{m/s^2},
\label{eq:disagreement}
\end{equation}
which approaches 2 when one sensor sees much more than the other. A pair is fused only if the two windows ended within half a window of each other; if one sensor produces nothing for 3\,s, the output falls back to the other and says so. A boot self-test checks the averaging, $D$, the rule that $R$ needs both sensors, rejection of a misaligned pair and the fallback. The equal-noise assumption has not been tested on the vehicle.

\section{Battery-management interface}
\label{sec:bms-interface}

The pack reports voltage, current, state of charge, capacity, temperatures and protection faults over Bluetooth Low Energy in the JBD protocol. An inherited implementation leaked a client object on every reconnection, reconnected on a fixed timer and framed messages by a terminator byte that can occur inside the data. The interface was rewritten with one reused client, reconnection only on a disconnect callback, framing from the declared length field, a checksum, and a plausibility check against a 72\,V pack. It searches every discovered service for the required characteristics instead of assuming the service, and logs every advertiser seen so that a wrong address is visible. The field offsets were confirmed byte for byte against an independently written parser in a teammate's code, which does not check the checksum.

Current status (11 October 2026). The checksum formula, taken from community reverse engineering, included the register byte, so every genuine frame failed by exactly three; firmware v0.5.4 corrected it. On the vehicle on 30 September the unit then stored 14 consecutive checksum-valid records over about 140\,s (74.78\,V, 0.00\,A, 60\%) before the connection was lost. The link is intermittent: the pack's signal was $-88$ to $-93$\,dBm, near the limit for holding a connection, so the unit may have to be mounted close to the pack. The reading has not been compared with a meter or the pack's app. The pack advertises as \texttt{DB24SA01L24S150ABU}, a 24-cell, 150\,A designation, while inherited documentation describes the main traction pack as lead-acid without a BMS, so this is probably a separate lithium auxiliary pack, which remains to be confirmed with the vehicle team. The earlier connection faults are in Table~\ref{tab:faults}.

\section{Positioning}
\label{sec:gnss-parsing-validation}
\label{sec:gnss-outdoor}

The modem's GNSS is polled every 3\,s with \texttt{AT+CGNSSINFO}. The parser was written defensively, checking the field count and logging the raw reply, because published reply formats differ between modem firmware versions \cite{simcoma76xx}. That paid off: the assumed 13-field reply was in fact 9 empty fields indoors, and on a rooftop on 29 and 30 September a fix gave 17 fields (and an 18-field variant while settling) in decimal degrees, so the first mapping had read satellite counts as the position. Since v0.5.3 the parser anchors on the latitude hemisphere letter and reads every field relative to it, which fits all observed layouts, and accepts a fix only if the layout and the coordinate, date and time formats are valid and the implied jump from the previous fix is under 100\,m/s; this rejected replies corrupted when a second query competed with the firmware's polling. A console command prints every raw reply so that the parser can be checked without extra commands. The same session found that the modem's power key toggles the modem, so the boot-time pulse switched a running modem off after every reprogramming; since v0.5.2 the key is pulsed only if the modem does not answer. After GNSS power-on, polls time out entirely for about 60 to 90\,s, so a test must allow this warm-up before recording. The speed field is in knots according to the manual \cite{simcoma76xx}, which has not yet been confirmed on the fitted modem. The reply layouts are in Appendix~\ref{app:devrecord}.

Firmware v0.7.3 adds assisted GNSS, which downloads orbit data over LTE every two hours (\texttt{AT+CAGPS} returned success on the bench), and, while there is no fix, the modem's network position (\texttt{AT+CLBS}) once a minute. Indoors this answered in about 0.7\,s with a reported accuracy of 550\,m, about 30\,m from the first outdoor fix at the same site; it is kept in separate fields, drawn on the map as an approximate circle, and never used for speed, heading or the ride index.

\section{Other vehicle inputs}
\label{sec:environmental-sensing}
\label{sec:hall-tap}

The Sensirion SEN55 reports particulate matter, humidity, temperature and VOC and NOx indices over I\textsuperscript{2}C; the gas indices are relative to recent background and take up to 1 and 6\,h respectively to meet specification after switch-on \cite{sensirionsen5x}, so gas indices from a short drive are indicative only. It was chosen so that conditions along a route can be recorded on the same timeline as the vehicle's measured response. It has been received but is not connected, for want of a six-way JST GH cable; the planned wiring is in Appendix~\ref{app:wiring}.

Motor speed would help separate powertrain vibration (RQ4). The Hall signal is referenced to the drivetrain, so it is to be tapped through a PC817 optocoupler module. Firmware v0.6.0 counts both edges in the hardware pulse counter with a 12.5\,$\mu$s glitch filter, so counting costs the IMU tasks nothing, and computes revolutions per minute as the edge rate divided by twice the pole-pair count, which is not yet known. Until the tap is wired the counter reads the sync pulse as a loopback, against which the count can be checked exactly: on 1 October it counted 666 edges in 333.3\,s, 2.00 edges per second, matching the 500\,ms toggle. Which Hall wire to tap, its voltage (5 or 12\,V) and the optocoupler module's input rating are still to be measured on the vehicle. The same firmware publishes the modem's signal quality every 10\,s so that failed publishes can be related to coverage.

\section{Camera subsystem}

\subsection{Capture and synchronisation}
\label{sec:sync-pulse}

The camera's first purpose is a visual record against which inertial events can be labelled, for example to tell a pothole from a deliberate manoeuvre. The camera and the inertial data are on different boards, so their clocks are related by a shared signal: a FreeRTOS task on the ESP32 toggles GPIO15 every 500\,ms with \texttt{vTaskDelayUntil} and logs each edge's microsecond time, and the Raspberry Pi~4 logs the arrival of each edge on GPIO17. On the ESP32 side successive logged edges were 500,000 to 500,001\,$\mu$s apart over an extended capture. The first Pi-side logger timestamped edges in a Python callback (\texttt{gpiozero}); its intervals clustered at 500.1 to 500.3\,ms with one unexplained 413.6\,ms gap. The edge logger was therefore rewritten (\texttt{CameraDetection/sync\_logger.py}) to take each edge's time from the kernel through the GPIO character device, on the same monotonic clock as the camera's frame timestamps, which narrowed the spread to under 0.7\,ms (Chapter~\ref{chap:results}); the camera library's \texttt{SensorTimestamp} gives the time at which the first pixel of each frame was read out, on that clock \cite{picamera2manual}. Frames and edges therefore share one time base. A 15\,s combined capture with \texttt{rpicam-vid} produced a consistent set (439 frames, about 29 frames per second at 1920$\times$1080, and 33 sync edges), but its frame times were relative to the first frame, not to the monotonic clock, which is why the offset had to be bounded separately (below). Two findings shaped this part. The pin first chosen for the pulse is not brought out on the board, which was caught against the manufacturer's schematic before any wiring. And twice the jumper on the pulse wire was disturbed by handling, once giving no edges and once a 16\,s burst of spurious edges: edges that do not fit the known 500\,ms pattern can be discarded in analysis, but a secured connector is needed on the vehicle. Before kernel timestamps were used, a lens-cover test bounded the offset between video and wall clock to under about 1\,s, a bound set by the human reporting method (Appendix~\ref{app:devrecord}); an LED driven from the sync pin would tighten it. Sub-second alignment is enough for labelling events after the fact.

\subsection{Detection}
\label{sec:hazard-detection}

The detector (\texttt{CameraDetection/hazard\_detector.py}) runs a pretrained SSD-MobileNet-v1, trained on COCO and quantised to 8-bit integers for LiteRT, on 640$\times$480 frames, which needs no training data and no accelerator. Only motor vehicles, motorcycles, bicycles, pedestrians and animals are reported, in three distance bands (immediate, warning, monitoring) estimated from the box width, an assumed width per class (for example 1.8\,m for a car) and the pinhole relation. The focal length is not yet calibrated, so the bands are indicative.

An alert is raised when a class appears in a band for three consecutive frames, re-raised if it holds a nearer band for three frames, and cleared only after five frames without it; within a frame the nearest detection of a class is used. The clearing rule came from the second live run, in which an alert that cleared on a single missed frame produced 16 activations in 47\,s for one person. The rules were checked offline by replaying scripted sequences through the unchanged loop, and later seen raising and clearing person alerts on live frames.

Data handling is minimal by design, and was fixed in code before the detector first processed live frames on 28 September 2026. Each alert records five fields: class, band, confidence, timestamp and alert status. Frames exist only in memory and are never written to disk; there is no face or number-plate recognition and no identification of anyone; inference is local; and the output is advisory, with no control of any vehicle function. The ride characterisation of Section~\ref{sec:ride-characterisation} uses no learned model.

Every frame's latency is measured from its \texttt{SensorTimestamp}: the time from readout to capture by the detector, preprocessing, inference, alert logic and the total, reported as median, 95th percentile and maximum every 5\,s and optionally written per frame. The measurement excludes the exposure time itself. Inference uses all four cores. The optional live view draws the detections on the image and serves it as MJPEG from its own thread, which always takes the newest frame, so a slow viewer cannot slow detection. Two input faults were found on the first live run: a placeholder entry in the label file shifted every class by one, and the camera's buffers are in blue, green, red order while the model expects red, green, blue \cite{picamera2manual}. The module's lack of an infrared-cut filter gave a pink cast; its own tuning file with saturation 1.8 restored the colours, and a correction for near-black pixels is applied to the live view only (Appendix~\ref{app:devrecord}).

Because the COCO classes include no road-surface hazards, a YOLO11n pothole model was fine-tuned in Colab, first on a public dataset and then on tiles cut from the road band of a South African dashcam dataset (Nienaber, Kroon and Booysen, Stellenbosch University); the data preparation is in Appendix~\ref{app:devrecord}. \outstanding{run of the pothole model on the Raspberry Pi~4 beside the main detector, and its held-out test score.}

\subsection{Range sensing and fusion}
\label{sec:tof-sensor}
\label{sec:camera-tof-fusion}

A VL53L5CX was chosen for proximity sensing because its 8$\times$8 zones in a 63\textdegree\ field separate left, centre and right from one device, where single-point sensors would have to be duplicated across directions. It involves no image and no learned model, so it works whatever the detector's state. It had not arrived by 30 September, so the three VL53L0X sensors of a UCT micromouse sensor board stand in, facing left, ahead and right, with a range of about 2\,m (1.2\,m used). They share one address, so the reader holds all three in shutdown and wakes each in turn to give it its own address (Appendix~\ref{app:wiring}). They are on the Raspberry Pi~4, which sits beside the camera and has a free bus, leaving the ESP32's acquisition undisturbed.

Finding: one loose sensor stopped all three. On 6 October every distance was null, because the start-up stopped at the first sensor that did not answer. The reader now skips a sensor that does not answer and logs which one, the bridge looks for it again every 60\,s, and since 10 October each I/O call has a 0.5\,s timeout so that a stuck sensor cannot freeze the reader; all three sensors were reading afterwards (\texttt{Planning/REMAINING\_WORK.md}).

The camera says what an object is and in which direction, but its distance is uncalibrated; each time-of-flight sensor measures distance well, but only close by, in one direction and without a class. A fusion step in the bridge (\texttt{CameraDetection/sensor\_fusion.py}) combines them. The detector serves each detection with a bearing computed from the box centre and the 53.5\textdegree\ horizontal field, and the bridge polls it five times a second. A detection is matched to a sensor when its bearing lies in that sensor's cone widened by a 5\textdegree\ gate and the sensor's nearest sample is within 200\,ms, valid and in range; the nearest reading wins and each sensor serves one detection. A matched detection becomes a fused hazard with the camera's class and the sensor's distance; an unmatched one keeps the camera estimate; a sensor with three valid in-range readings and no match reports an unclassified obstacle; and camera data older than 1\,s are ignored, so with the detector stopped only obstacles are reported. Hazards are sorted nearest first. The sensor directions ($-35$, 0 and $+35$\textdegree, 12.5\textdegree\ half-angle) are defaults, not measurements, and with them the side cones ($-47.5$ to $-22.5$\textdegree\ and 22.5 to 47.5\textdegree) overlap the camera's $\pm 26.75$\textdegree\ view only at its outer edges (Figure~\ref{fig:fusion-geometry}), so most objects to the side can be reported only by a time-of-flight sensor, without a class. A sensor reports the nearest surface in its cone, which need not be the object the camera saw, and there is no tracking. A continuous record of distances and bearings would collect more than the system needs, so the fusion log is off by default and is switched on only for a validation session, and then holds numbers only, no images. The bench validation (test C9) first calibrates the camera axis, field of view and each sensor's cone with a narrow target on a marked floor, then checks a person crossing at 1\,m, a person standing at 0.5, 1.0 and 1.5\,m against a tape, a box outside the detector's classes at 0.8\,m (to be reported as a time-of-flight obstacle) and a person at 2.5\,m, beyond the sensors' range (camera only).

\subsection{Dashboard integration}
\label{sec:dashboard-page}
\label{sec:dashboard-link}
\label{sec:live-only}
\label{sec:pi5-default}

An inspection of the inherited PySide6 dashboard on 2 October found no live input: every page ran on simulated data, with one unused entry point for a live vehicle state and a fallback to simulation after 2.5\,s without one. A driver's display should not depend on coverage or a remote broker, so a local path was built. Firmware v0.7.0 prints one compact JSON line a second on USB; a bridge on the Raspberry Pi~4 (\texttt{telemetry\_bridge.py}) reads it with the time-of-flight distances and the fused hazards and serves them over HTTP; and a live-data provider on the Raspberry Pi~5 polls the bridge every 0.5\,s. The dashboard therefore needs one network link for camera, alerts and telemetry. It finds the Raspberry Pi~4 on any network from a beacon that the bridge broadcasts every 2\,s, with localhost, an override, a wired address, mDNS and the last good address as alternatives (Figure~\ref{fig:pi4-discovery}, Appendix~\ref{app:devrecord}). The design addresses the dashboard issues of Section~\ref{sec:lit-display} as follows.

Data validity and update age. A live-only mode (\texttt{TUKZIE\_LIVE\_ONLY=1}) stops the simulation entirely. When the bridge cannot be reached, or the telemetry unit's record is more than 3\,s old, the provider stops sending, and within 2.5\,s the dashboard shows an explicit no-data state: every sensor field empty, every validity flag false, ``-{}-'' or ``Unavailable'' on the pages, and a message that live data was lost. A field with no live source (speed, until the GNSS units are confirmed; pedals; temperatures) stays empty rather than taking a default, and the state of charge stays empty until the BMS reports. This mode was added after two findings: when the Raspberry Pi~4 changed network, the dashboard fell back to simulated values with no sign, and a battery level of 82\% was shown that was the simulator's last value, not a measurement.

Layout. The changes add few elements: a camera page with the live view and the active alerts; a Sensors page with the three distances as gauges, the fused hazards nearest first, and the battery, position, motor and ride readings; a ride-quality card on the diagnostics page; and the driving page's front-obstacle distance, taken from the forward sensor only so that a car parked alongside does not raise a front warning.

Contrast and colour. Alerts and gauges use the dashboard's own status colours (amber under 1.0\,m, red under 0.5\,m), so the team's visual language is kept. Contrast in daylight has not been measured.

Alert priority. An immediate hazard from the fusion, or loss of the Raspberry Pi~4 link for 5\,s after it was working, raises a banner over every page; touching it opens the Sensors page, and a button hides it for 30\,s. Lower bands appear only on the camera and Sensors pages.

Interaction. Finger-drag scrolling was added and developer options were moved behind one switch. Touch response was reported as slow, so the touch controller was read directly during 25 taps (Chapter~\ref{chap:results}).

The integration is applied as a patch set of 63 edits to a separate copy of the team's dashboard by a deploy script that refuses to touch the team's folder and writes nothing unless every edit's anchor text occurs exactly once, so the team's code is never changed. Since 6 October a system service starts the integrated copy at boot on the Raspberry Pi~5 in place of the team's service, which one command restores. The camera page uses only Qt's networking and widget modules, because the dashboard's web-engine map crashed the program on the Raspberry Pi~5; a street-tile map drawn directly with Qt replaces it. The map and navigation work is summarised in Appendix~\ref{app:devrecord}.

\section{Communications and local logging}
\label{sec:mqtt-networking}

Every 10\,s the modem task publishes a compact JSON summary of the ride, BMS and GNSS state over MQTT using the modem's own \texttt{AT+CMQTT} commands. Publishing is kept inside the modem task because the modem UART has no lock, and a second task issuing commands would corrupt both streams. The broker is a public, unauthenticated test broker (\texttt{test.mosquitto.org}), used only to prove the path and not as a destination for real vehicle data; the credentials of the vacation-work team's own broker were deliberately not copied into this project's code. For the start, connect and publish commands the modem returns \texttt{OK} and then a separate result line whose code gives success or failure \cite{simcoma76xx}; since v0.5.0 the firmware waits for that line rather than trusting the \texttt{OK}. A session is treated as lost after three failed publishes or when the modem reports a dropped client or lost network; the old session is then shut down in the order the manual requires, and retries start at 30\,s and double to at most 5 minutes. Since v0.5.3 the firmware also reconnects as soon as the modem reports that packet data is available again. Console commands report the state, switch logging off and on, force a reconnection, dump or clear the log, and erase the calibration.

\label{sec:local-logging}
Each record is also written to a LittleFS file system in the 3.5\,MB data partition of the internal flash (Table~\ref{tab:config}), whether or not MQTT is connected, so that a session leaves a complete record without coverage. Two findings shaped this part.

Finding: flash writes pause acquisition. With a write every 10\,s, both IMUs showed a 33 to 40\,ms gap at each write, although they are on separate buses; switching logging off for 120\,s, with publishing continuing, removed the gaps. Writing to flash suspends code executing from flash on both cores, which explains why both stalled together. Since v0.6.0 records are buffered in memory (8\,KB) and written in one append about once a minute. This reduces how often the pause occurs, not its length, and up to a minute of records is lost if power is cut before a flush.

Finding: missing values made invalid JSON. Reading the whole log back through a paced, indexed dump (\texttt{BenchTest/log\_dump.py}) showed that most records contained \texttt{nan} for fields with no value, such as the position before a fix, which JSON does not allow; the same text had been published. Watching the live stream had not shown it. Since v0.6.3 such fields are written as \texttt{null}.

The log is capped at 3\,MB and then truncated rather than rotated, and it does not yet record which lines have been published, so the backlog is not replayed after an outage. The file survives reprogramming and grew by about 160\,KB an hour on the bench, so it must be read back and cleared before a long session such as the road test; after the read-back of 1 October the copy was saved in the repository and the board's file cleared. \outstanding{central database ingest on the Raspberry Pi~5, sequence numbers and backlog replay (firmware v0.7.4), with the synchronisation measurements: completeness, delay and duplicates.}

\subsection{Bench tools}
\label{sec:bench-tools}

Tools were written so that each test leaves a record that can be checked later. A browser dashboard reads the telemetry unit's serial output over USB through the Web Serial interface, with no installation, and shows the fused and per-sensor ride features, each IMU's rate and drop count, GNSS and BMS status, free memory and the boot self-tests, with rolling plots of vibration and disagreement (Figure~\ref{fig:live-dashboard}, Appendix~\ref{app:figures}). It can replay a saved log through the same parsing, so a recorded session is reviewed exactly as it appeared live. The subsystem pages were later combined into one bench console that also shows the camera's live view and alerts, sends the firmware's console commands, filters the log by subsystem and saves timestamped sessions; it only reads data, and was checked by replaying the recorded session (Figure~\ref{fig:console-replay}, Appendix~\ref{app:evidence}). Scripted tests drive the firmware and record the result: \texttt{BenchTest/v050\_bench\_test.py} (commands, logging and reconnection), \texttt{BenchTest/signal\_loss\_test.py} (radio off for 60\,s), \texttt{BenchTest/log\_dump.py} (full log read-back) and \texttt{BenchTest/sw7\_check.py} (pre-test check of every link). Offline tests replace the camera, model or bridge with stand-ins so that the detector's alert rules, the fusion and the dashboard pages can be checked without hardware.

\subsection{Integration and power}
\label{sec:full-system-integration}

All telemetry-unit subsystems were first run together on the target board, and then the whole chain from the telemetry unit through the Raspberry Pi~4 to the dashboard on the Raspberry Pi~5. Finding: the Raspberry Pi~4 ran under-voltage and throttled to 600\,MHz through much of the bench work, and on 6 October stopped booting. A logger of its power and temperature flags every 2\,s showed the flags clear with nothing attached, and under-voltage starting 14\,s after the telemetry unit was plugged into its USB port, when the LTE modem came up. The modem's transmit current through the Pi's USB port is the cause, so the telemetry unit must have its own supply, for example a powered hub. The same log showed the Pi~4 reaching its soft temperature limit with the detector running and no cooling, and a fan was fitted (Appendix~\ref{app:wiring}).

\subsection{Vehicle tests}
\label{sec:field-test-design}

The vehicle integration and test plan (SW7-VIT-001) divides the vehicle work into a stationary phase with the drive wheel raised and a phase with the vehicle on the ground. The stationary phase covers mounting, calibration in the mounted position, acquisition timing, synchronisation, the Hall tap and pole-pair count, a powertrain vibration baseline with the motor run at low, medium and high settings for about 60\,s each, BMS voltage against a meter, GNSS and LTE static tests and an induced outage. The ground phase covers repeatability over repeated traversals, speed dependence, surface discrimination, sensor placement, coast-down separation of the powertrain, and payload. Sessions with the 72\,V system energised require a second person, rated stands and chocks; all mounting is removable, with no drilling or permanent modification, and the vehicle can be returned to standard in about 20 minutes (R13). \outstanding{road test of 14 October 2026: protocol, route, conditions, mounting positions and the sessions completed.}
